Suppose I build a program, a database of drinks.\
The software contains thousands of cocktail "recipes", and one of the
product's innovative tricks is that you enter the liquor you have in
your bar and the program suggests drinks you could make with what you
have (okay, now I'm thirsty).

I can do several things with this product.

1.  Put the "Coctelera" program on a CD-ROM and sell it through
    traditional channels, get paid licenses for it, deal with a big
    distributor and get filthy rich off the thousands of copies I'll
    sell. This is the Microsoft model, closed, proprietary (or
    "privative", as some say). I call it the traditional model of
    selling software.

2.  Release the program's source, coctelera.sourceforge.net, but not
    the database, which is sold through subscriptions that let you keep
    it up to date. This model is interesting: open source for the
    programs, but not for the data. I haven't seen this model; if
    anyone knows an example of this kind, I'd like to hear about it.

3.  Release the program's source and the contents of the database
    (coctelera.gnu.org). In this case there are no barriers to entry,
    so what's left for me is to build a services structure around it,
    providing database maintenance services, for example. If you're
    interested in getting filthy rich, this won't do; stick with option
    0. In this case the program is free and so is the data, so everyone
    can enjoy the drinks, cheers! It's what the FSF and open source do.
    Can you make money? Yes, you could make decent money tailoring the
    program to restaurants and bars and charging them licenses for that
    commercial version. Or you could package it on a CD-ROM and sell it
    in supermarkets at a low price.

4.  The third way (yes, I cheated by numbering from zero ;).\
    Put the system on a website, www.coctelera.com, charge for
    advertising, and everybody has access to it. They can even get
    access via WAP, and I sign a contract with the phone companies. In
    this case whether the code is released or not makes no difference,
    because my business is in the service I'm providing on top of the
    program and the database. This is Google's case.

You get filthy rich all the same, but you have a better image, and you
take care to keep that image polished by offering new and innovative
services (Coctelera Bar, Coctelera Desktop, Coctelera Appliance,
Coctelera Mail, etc.)

And here comes the paradigm shift in the software business that is
happening before our eyes.\
A shift that Microsoft doesn't understand, and that they don't
understand much on the other side either.
