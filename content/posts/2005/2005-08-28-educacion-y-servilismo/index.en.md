I'm rereading [Fernando Savater](http://www.epdlp.com/escritor.php?id=2277),
specifically El valor de educar (The Value of Educating), where he
mentions that:

> According to Jaime Balmes's well-known dictum, the art of teaching
> how to learn consists of building factories, not warehouses.

But how much is education and how much is instruction? I'm afraid there
is a feeling that development comes from training more technicians, for
which all we need are instructors, not educators.

Things aren't as simple as the [Castalia blog](http://www.atinachile.cl/node/3607) puts it, in the sense that
we should question the existence of the humanities-and-science high
schools (an outdated and inappropriate classification, since as far as
I know scientists are human too).

The truth is that if people from this kind of high school have nowhere
to continue their education, that's a serious problem, because it means
we have a society that places little value on developing higher skills
and prefers the immediacy of what is wrongly called practical
knowledge.

I'm not saying it's bad to train technicians, but really, the greater
value lies in forming people able to face the present and future world
with adequate intellectual tools. People who know how to think, and not
just mechanics who are experts in technologies that go obsolete every 6
months.

First, there isn't enough room for that many technicians, because our
countries never did industrialize. Second, if we are heading toward a
knowledge society, we need highly prepared people, and a
humanities-and-science high school doesn't provide that, much less a
vocational one.

What's going on is that Chile and many Latin American countries have
chosen to follow the path of the so-called service economy (not to say
the servility economy), where the skills kids leave the
humanities-and-science high schools with seem to be enough (except that
we have to give them a coat of English, and in the future of Mandarin)
