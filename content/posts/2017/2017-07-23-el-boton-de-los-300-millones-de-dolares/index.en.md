Sometimes we engineers are so focused on functionality that we don't
bother with usability. Let me see if I can explain:  
[we put the focus on defining the use case](/blog/lnds/2013/03/23/expectativas), we concentrate
on giving users what they ask for, but not what they need.

But do we ever observe how users actually use our
system? I have several stories about how we came to realize that
our users were doing manual work to fit their input to
our systems, work that wouldn't have been necessary if they had
described to us how the information really arrived, instead of asking for an
ideal format of how it was supposedly meant to arrive. 

That's why I'm set on getting the developer/programmer
closer to interacting directly with the user. [What's more, I've always
thought it's a good idea for programmers to use the systems
they build under the same conditions the user has. That's how
you discover bugs, or incomplete or inadequate
features.

I'm going to tell you a story, one that's famous among the people who
work in usability engineering (yes, there's an engineering discipline for
that and it's very important). 

It's the story of the three hundred million dollar button.

The article was published by Jared Spool in 2009, and you can read it at
this link: <https://articles.uie.com/three_hund_million_button>.

What follows is a translation of sorts of the original article (Spool
includes a link to a Spanish translation in his post, but
frankly I found it very bad; fair warning, though, this is neither
a literal nor a complete translation):

## How changing a button increased a site's annual revenues by $300 million

It's hard to imagine a form that could be simpler: two fields, two buttons,
and one link. Yet it turns out this form was preventing
customers from purchasing products from a major e-commerce site,
to the tune of US\$ 300,000,000 a year. What was even worse: the
designers of the site had no clue this was a problem.

The form in question had the fields Email Address
and Password. The buttons were
Login and Register. The link was Forgot
Password. It was the login form for the
site. A form users encounter all the time. How
could they have problems with it?

The problem wasn't so much the form's layout as
the way it behaved. Users would run into it after
they had filled their shopping cart with the products they wanted to
purchase, at the moment they pressed the Checkout button.  This login
form came up before they could enter their payment
information.

The team figured this form enabled repeat customers to
purchase faster. First-time customers wouldn't mind
registering because, after all, they would come back for more and appreciate the
expediency in subsequent purchases. Everybody wins,
right?

# "I'm not here to be in a relationship"

Usability tests were conducted with people who needed to buy
products from the site. They were asked to bring their shopping lists and
were even given the money to make the purchases. All they
had to do was complete the purchase.

The first thing discovered was how wrong the team was about
first-time shoppers. They did mind registering.
They resented having to register when they encountered the page. In the
words of one shopper: "I'm not here to enter into a relationship. I just
want to buy something."

Some first-time shoppers couldn't remember if it was their first time, and
became frustrated as each common email and password
combination failed. The observers were surprised by how much they resisted
registering.

Without knowing what was involved in registration, all the
users who clicked the button did so with a sense of
despair. Many vocalized that the only thing the site's owners wanted
was to get their information to pester them later with
marketing messages they didn't want. Some imagined nefarious purposes
and an obvious attempt to invade their privacy. (In reality, the site
asked nothing during registration that it didn't need to complete the
purchase: name, shipping address, billing address,
and payment information.)

## Not so good for repeat customers either

Repeat customers weren't happy either. Except for a few
who remembered their login information, many got stuck on the
form. They couldn't remember the email address or
password they had used. Remembering which email they had
registered with was problematic: many had multiple email
addresses or had changed them over the years.

When a customer couldn't remember the email and password, they'd
try guessing them multiple times. These guesses rarely
succeeded. Some would eventually click the link to recover the
password, which becomes another problem if you can't remember which
email address you originally registered with.

[After this study, an analysis of the retailer's database
was carried out, only to discover that 45% of all customers
had multiple registrations in the system, some as many as 10. They also
analyzed how many people requested password recovery, and found
it reached more than 160,000 per day. 75% of these people never
tried to complete the purchase.]{style="letter-spacing: 0.01rem;"}

The form, intended to make shopping easier, turned out to
help only a small percentage of the customers who encountered it.
(Even many of those customers didn't get much help, since
it took them extra effort to update incorrect information,
like changed addresses or credit cards.) Contrary to
expectations, the form was preventing sales, a great many
sales.

## The US\$300,000,000 fix

The designers fixed the problem simply. They took away the
Register button. In its place they put a *Continue*
button, with a simple message: *"You do not need to create an account
to make purchases on our site. Simply click
Continue to proceed to checkout. To make your future purchases even
faster, you can create an account during checkout."*

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/9f6fe56c-6fba-11e7-976c-0d1ee2977bbe-aa9f18b7)

The results: the number of customers purchasing went up by 45%. The
extra purchases resulted in 15 million dollars the first month. In that
first year the site took in an additional US\$300,000,000.

The author says that on his answering machine he still keeps
the message he received from the CEO of this retailer. It's a very
simple message: "Spool! You\'re the man!" It didn't
need to be a more complex message. After all, they had only changed
a button.
