[Francotirador publishes](http://www.elfrancotirador.cl/2009/02/04/como-si-fuera-cierto/)
a [recent Juanelo strip](http://www.juanelo.cl/2009/02/juanelo-874/), along with a
reference to a
[news story](http://archives.cnn.com/2002/SHOWBIZ/Music/09/23/uk.silence/)
from 2002 about a lawsuit brought against the English composer Mike Batt
for including a piece of 1 minute of silence on his album.

Batt was accused of plagiarizing John Cage's work 4´33´´.

The lawsuit wasn't filed by John Cage, of course, but by the lawyers in
charge of the John Cage Trust, since the American composer had died in
1992.

An interesting character, this [John Cage](http://ronsen.org/cagelinks.html), quite a controversial composer
in his day, a man who experimented with aleatoric music, electronic
music and the non-standard use of musical instruments. Besides being a
composer, he was a painter, poet, philosopher and mycologist. That last
one is probably where he sampled more than one hallucinogenic specimen,
which would explain the trips he went on musically ;)

In 1948 Cage visits a university and enters an [anechoic
chamber](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A1mara_anecoica), a room
designed to absorb all sound. [According to
Wikipedia](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage):

> Cage entered the chamber expecting to hear silence, but he later
> wrote: "I heard two sounds, one high and one low. When I described
> them to the engineer in charge, he informed me that the high one was
> my nervous system in operation, the low one my blood in circulation."

Cage had gone to a place where he expected no sound, and even so a
sound could still be made out. Faced with this astonishing fact he
wrote: "Until I die there will be sounds. And they will continue
following my death. One need not fear about the future of music".

(How I wish the people who alarm us about the supposed death of music
would read what John Cage said)

This incident, which made him realize the impossibility of silence (and
therefore that it is impossible to kill music), was what inspired his
most notorious "composition", 4′33″, four minutes and thirty-three
seconds of "silence".

I've seen references to this work in movies, and you can watch
[this video](http://www.ubu.com/film/cage_433.html) of the piece being
performed by the BBC Symphony Orchestra in London.

It's controversial, and it challenges the definition of what music is.
Even so, someone was accused of plagiarizing it, and had to pay a
six-figure sum to make the little problem go away.

So now you know: even silence is protected by copyright, although I
suspect John Cage wouldn't much agree with that.
