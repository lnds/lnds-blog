> "Talk is cheap, show me the code" -- Linus Torvalds

I don't talk much about my job on this blog, it's a personal policy,
but I will mention that a few months ago I had the chance to
implement a mission-critical service in which every component
is backed by open source products, from the operating system
(RedHat) and the middleware (JBoss) to the database (Postgres).

I think we did a good job with the help of our technology
vendors, and along the way we laid the foundations of the
basic service infrastructure (SOA) for our company, which will guide our
future development over the coming years.

As [Franco Catrin says](http://www.fayerwayer.com/2009/08/vida-mas-alla-del-sistema-operativo-en-red-hat/),
choosing base components, such as the operating system, is a
tactical decision, but as we move up the scale of
complexity of IT solutions, the decision to use open source
or not becomes a strategic one.

These decisions should not be made on the criterion of saving on
licenses. In fact, maintenance licenses for Redhat Linux
Enterprise usually cost the same as, and sometimes more than, a version of
Windows. License cost is not the relevant factor in these
cases. Whoever makes technology decisions based on the cost of
licenses is a mouse.

> "Open source is not something to be feared. Open source is something to
> be explained. Open source wins not because it's open and not
> because it's free. Open source wins only when it's better" 
> -- [Larry Ellison](/en/blog/lnds/2007/08/17/free-software-is-no-threat/)

Larry Ellison is obsessed with winning, and as such he believes in the concepts of better
and worse. Since I don't believe in those terms, I think in evolutionary
terms, and evolution is adaptation to change.

What kind of software is fitter (in the evolutionary sense)?

Biology teaches us that the richest ecosystems, with large
populations, react faster to change, among other reasons
because the gene pool is more diverse.

In the case of software, the genome, the DNA, is the code, and the gene
pool, the ecosystem that sustains evolution, is given by the
number of programmers who have access to that code, that is, the
developer community (I leave out of this discussion the user
community, which tends to be larger for proprietary software).

So, for certain kinds of applications, it is better to have access
to the code, and to the ecosystem that sustains it. Open source gives access to
that; proprietary software makes it harder.

Could these processes have been implemented with proprietary (non
open source) tools? Yes, definitely. So how do you make this decision?

I am a techno-agnostic. I have had the chance to take part in
deciding the technology strategy at 3 companies, and I have
used both proprietary and open technologies.

This time I believe I made the most important technology decision
of my professional life, both for the size of the service
and for its real impact. This time I went with open source, because I
truly need to be flexible and adapt to change.

I work in a heavily regulated industry (the pension
industry), and because of its dynamics, and its social impact, it is subject to
major changes and reforms coming from the government and the
legislature. It was important to be in a position to withstand those
abrupt changes, to act with flexibility. That was my criterion, and
open software gives me that flexibility.

Strategic decisions in technology, like any strategic
decision, must be reasoned. They should not be made because of fashion, or for
personal political reasons. It is not a matter of fundamentalisms, or of
[stale pseudo-ethics](/en/blog/lnds/2009/06/18/ethics-the-case-of-the-hackers/).
For me a decision of this kind is a matter of evolution (and
therefore, it is also an ethical decision).
