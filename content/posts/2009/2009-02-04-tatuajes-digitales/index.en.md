> The generation coming up now is the first with the ability to
> publicly record anything they want to record. Wonderful as that is, it
> gives me pause. This coming generation will be the first that has to
> chew on this issue, and frankly I don't envy them. There are a few
> things (come on, we all have them) that I might have written about, or
> felt passionately about, at 18 that I don't want publicly available at
> 35, or 50. Much of our growth as people and as thinkers comes from
> trying out new ideas and making some mistakes. For many of us, that
> growth is preserved only in our memories or those of the people close
> to us, in letters, and in journals stacked in a garage. What we
> publish digitally, I think, is like a tattoo: it sticks, publicly,
> forever. There is a reason many of us are discouraged from getting
> tattoos until we reach adulthood. The tattoo of our favorite cartoon
> character may be awesome at 19, but not so much later on. I hope the
> web encourages a great deal of public thought and expression. It is
> also on us to be aware of the public nature and the longevity of what
> we put out there, considering that what we leave behind stays with us
> as the equivalent of a digital tattoo we thought we wanted, but
> actually didn't.


[Stephanie Schippe](http://www.stephanieschipper.com/)r, in her very
interesting
[reflection](http://www.technologyfortherestof.us/2008/12/cognitive-surplus-and-digital-tatoos.html)
(which she wrote after reading [Gin, Television, and Social
Surplus](/en/blog/lnds/2008/05/28/gin-television-and-social-surplus/)
by Clay Shirky).

We all have plenty of things we've published in forums, mailing lists,
blogs, social networks, that we later just have to own, nothing more.
I can trace contributions of mine on Google back to 1993, when there
was barely a web to write on!

I don't know whether this business of leaving a record will be worse
for the young than for the old. Sure, a fifteen-year-old girl might
look at her Facebook record at fifty and be horrified, or maybe not, it
might turn out to be something wonderful, what do I know.

What is clear is that you can't take back what you left on the record,
and it's better to own it.

It's worth reading [Stephanie Schippe's post](http://www.technologyfortherestof.us/2008/12/cognitive-surplus-and-digital-tatoos.html)r,
and reflecting on these matters. I don't know what you think; maybe Pepe
Flores is right, and with the Internet privacy is over...
