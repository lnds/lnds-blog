> I was born in '67\
> The year of Sgt. Pepper\
> And are you experienced


As time goes by I find it easier and easier to accept Turing's idea
that there is no great difference between us and machines. Or, to put
it another way, the gap between machines and us keeps getting smaller.
And you realize this as you grow old. Because, just as with machines,
time does its part, and you suffer the same deterioration any artifact
goes through.


Entropy is unforgiving. The pipes clog up, the gears wear down, and the
movements that used to be quicker grow more unhurried. Your face and
your hair give it away. It is something you can't deny, however much
you'd like to.

> Into a suburban heaven\
> Yeah it should\'ve been forever\
> It all seems to make so much sense

I am not the same one who once played in the hills of Chuquicamata, or
who studied near Parque O'Higgins in Santiago. But I am still the same
person. I am the sum of all my changes, and the result of all my
experiences.


Heraclitus said that *"no man ever steps in the same river twice"*, and
Parmenides worked very hard to prove him wrong. That is why we
Westerners have this idea that our essence does not change. Because the
Eleatic philosopher worked very hard to convince us that we are an
immutable, perfect and indestructible unity. Some called that the soul,
and Plato along with Aristotle named it being (*ontos*), that which
defines us.

The thing is, everything around you keeps changing, and if you only
play the role of observer, it seems that the world is transformed, but
you are not.

> But after a while\
> You realize time flies\
> And the best thing that you can do\
> Is take whatever comes to you\
> 'Cause time flies

In Isaac Asimov's story The Bicentennial Man, the robot Andrew
Martin[^1] observes the world around him in an exceptional and unique
way, and witnesses its continuous change across eight generations, a
transformation that he himself brings about.

In fifty-two years I have witnessed astonishing changes. Not many have
gone the way I expected as a kid, but they still haven't stopped
surprising me. Although sometimes you feel that you don't change, the
truth is that a little honesty forces you to admit that even your own
ideas and values have undergone some mutation.


> She said luck is what you make it\
> You just reach out and take it\
> Now let's dance a while

I don't believe in luck, and yet I believe I have been fortunate. In
the Carmina Burana an anonymous monk, many years ago, left us the
fundamental observation: *"O Fortuna, velut luna statu variabilis"*.

That's how it is: today fortune smiles on me, but maybe next year I
will have reason to lament. But all in all, and in spite of the
variations, I have to say the balance is positive.

Maybe that is the indestructible essence we carry inside, the balance
of everything good and bad and the feeling of satisfaction, or misery,
that the result leaves us with.

> She said nothing ever happens\
> If you don\'t make it happen\
> And if you can\'t laugh and smile

I say that if in the end you can't laugh, or celebrate, and you look
back and no smile appears on your face, then it is because you haven't
had a full life. Nothing happens if you don't let it happen.


Woody Allen once said that *"comedy is tragedy plus time"*. And it is
one of the few things on which I think he is completely right. In the
end, when the Grim Reaper comes, let him find you smiling; that way you
may manage to throw him off and he'll let you stay a while longer. At
least those are my plans. If you can't beat them, confuse them.

> And laughing in the summer showers\
> That's still the way I see you now


Always remember the laughter of children. Your own laughter, or your
baby's when they laughed for the first time. What a glorious moment
that is!


I hope to enjoy my grandchildren's laughter in the same way, and that
these rusty mechanisms that make up my worn-out body hold on long
enough to see them play and run around my yard.

> How does time break down\
> With no marker, things slow down.\
> A conference of the strange\
> And your family is deranged

It wasn't until after forty that I began to expose myself more to the
world and pass on my experience. I once wrote that this was liberating.
First by writing on this blog, and later by giving talks and taking
part in conferences. Even publishing books.


My family was always with me, one way or another. I always felt their
support and trust. You must first put your house in order before going
out to explore the world.

Without meaning to, I ended up following Montaigne's path. In "El
Legado de Occidente" (The Legacy of the West), the great Stefan Zweig
tells how the philosopher from Bordeaux shut himself away for a decade
in his marvelous tower, surrounded by his books and his musings, but
after a while, as if he were a philosophical cicada, he went out into
the world to travel and test all his ideas against reality.

> I could tell you what I'm thinking\
> While we sit here drinking\
> But I'm not sure where to start

You never become wise in the solitude of introspection.

Just as happened to Montaigne, "I do not find myself when I look for
myself the hardest. I find myself by surprise when I least expect it."

Nothing gives you more chances to find yourself than untangling what
there is of you reflected in others. That is why I learned, quite late
in life, to cultivate friendship and conversation, with young and old.
To get to know others and test my reflections against them, even if
only in cyberspace. Although it is better if you share a glass of
wine.

I wonder whether Montaigne would have been a blogger, had he been born
in this age. I want to believe so, and I allow myself the arrogance of
feeling he is a colleague and a teacher. *"The surest sign of wisdom is
a constant cheerfulness."*

> You see there's something wrong here\
> I'm sorry if I'm not clear
> Can you stop smoking your cigar
>
> And the coat you wore to Alton Towers\
> Is still the way I see you now

I am a bundle of contradictions. I want to believe in the purity,
perfection and indestructibility of Parmenides' being. But I am aware
of Heraclitus' continuous change. That is why I prefer to say "estoy
feliz" rather than "soy feliz" (Spanish has two verbs for "to be": one
for the state you are in, one for what you are), because I don't think
we are; we are merely here, in this world. I believe in the fire of
Heraclitus, in eternal change, and that to understand anything you
have to appreciate movement; I resist static analysis. And I also
bought Turing's idea. I am just a machine in the process of
decomposing. But sometimes, when I get introspective, like today, I
prefer to believe that I am unique, indestructible and always the
same. May Heraclitus forgive me this small betrayal, but it must be
the special mood of a special day.

Maybe I am musing too much today, on my birthday. Forgive me for
distracting you with these reflections, somewhat improvised and full
of references, some of them even obscure.

*"The wrinkles of the spirit make us older than those of the face"*,
Montaigne again.

The thing is, reflecting on your age can take you to unknown places,
even negative ones, so we had better back up.

*"Books are the best provision I have found for this human journey"*,
says the French philosopher. I would add music. Because in Montaigne's
day they didn't have progressive rock, so we live in a better age. We
can express our thoughts with the help of others (*"I quote others
only in order to better express my own thought"*).
