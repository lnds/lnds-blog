A few years ago I heard someone who held a senior position in
technology declare that their highest aspiration was to be able to sleep
soundly, secure in the knowledge that every process and system was
running without problems.

I don't agree with that; I think it's a futile ambition. It's the same
thing that happens with people who only want [to be happy](/en/blog/lnds/2010/10/02/ser-and-estar/). 
Those aspirations strike me as childish, and they keep you from growing,
from developing. But in the world of business, and of the technology
that supports the business, that outlook can even be counterproductive.

"Well, you can't never let anything happen to him. Then nothing would
ever happen to him," Dory wisely told Marlin. Living sheltered, hoping
nothing happens to us, seems to me the worst way to face life. 

Consider a fragile object, a vase of very fine porcelain, for example.
What do we care about when it comes to this object? What we want is to
look after it, we want nothing to happen to it, we wish it order,
tranquility and, above all, predictability. An earthquake or a
hyperactive child are a fragile object's worst enemies.

Everything that abhors volatility abhors stressors. Harm, chaos,
unexpected events, disorder, unforeseen consequences, uncertainty and,
above all, time. Like those executives who want to sleep soundly.

But what is the opposite of fragile?

Robust? Resilient?

Well, no, because the robust withstands the blows and is still there;
it takes a shock and stays the same. A resilient person adapts to
difficult situations, but doesn't necessarily overcome them, or confront
them in order to change or eliminate them \[1\].

The opposite of fragile would be something that not only resists, but
also improves in the process. There is no proper term for this concept,
and that's why Nassim Nicholas Taleb calls it antifragile\[2\].

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/634b7924-6118-11e8-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7)

Antifragility is a property we find in everything that changes over
time. It's in revolutions, technological innovation, the survival of a
company, antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and it explains our success as a
species.

What doesn't kill you makes you stronger, said Nietzsche, and with that
line he was pointing out one of the key characteristics of antifragile
systems.

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/8e6ede86-6118-11e8-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7)

Antifragility is a relative property, depending on the domain. The
usual example is the boxer, who may be healthy and robust in his
physical condition and may get better with every new fight, but may be
emotionally fragile and cry when he breaks up with his girlfriend.

Things, like our precious porcelain vase, cannot be antifragile,
because in the presence of stressors (like an earthquake, or the
passing of time) they collapse and break, and they can't repair
themselves.

Living beings and complex systems, on the other hand, behave very
differently. They are aggregates of components that interact with one
another and exchange information by means of stressors. That is why they
can become antifragile.

Our muscles and bones get stronger in the presence of stressors, like
gravity and exercise. Just as our muscles atrophy if we spend too long
in bed, antifragile systems deteriorate in the absence of stressors.

Another common example is language. Nobody learns their mother tongue
by taking grammar exams. On the contrary, we learn by imitating our
elders and making mistakes. We all laugh, in a kind way, at little
kids' mistakes, but that helps them improve. Besides, it's when we're in
tough situations where we need to communicate that we learn a language
best.

The problem is that we have structured the modern world in such a way
that we are damaging the capacity for antifragility of many of our
systems. According to Taleb, "this is the tragedy of modernity: as with
neurotically overprotective parents, those trying to help are often
hurting us the most".

Why does this happen? According to Taleb it's due to what he calls
"causal opacity". What happens is that the exchange of information
between the components of a complex system is much more intricate than
we suspect. It is very hard to see the arrow that links cause and
consequence. This opacity makes ordinary logic and a large share of the
conventional methods of analysis inapplicable. In a complex system we
can't limit ourselves to isolating a single causal relationship. 

Another problem is that logic, by definition, excludes nuance. But the
truth lies in the nuances, therefore logic is a useless instrument for
finding the truth, especially in what we call the social sciences.

Another cause is that this idea, that systems may need a bit of stress
and agitation, has been ignored by those who grasp it in one domain but
not in another. Some people can understand an idea in a domain like
medicine and fail to recognize it in another like economics.


For Taleb, one example is economic crises. Since market-regulating
policies seek to fix prices or eliminate speculators, and therefore the
moderate volatility they cause, an illusion of stability is produced,
with periods of calm interrupted by sudden jumps. Since the participants
aren't used to volatility, these episodes cause panic. "When a currency
never varies, a slight, very slight move makes people believe that the
world is ending." For Taleb, injecting a little confusion into the
system, on a permanent basis, ends up stabilizing the system,
paradoxical as it may seem.


Innovation and sophistication arise from an initial situation of
necessity, which is answered with something that goes far beyond
satisfying that necessity. We have plenty of sayings, like "Necessity is
the mother of invention", or "hunger sharpens the wits". For Taleb,
"**The excess energy released from overreaction to setbacks is what
innovates!**"


> "It contradicts modern ideas of innovation and progress on many
> levels, as we tend to think that innovation comes from bureaucratic
> funding, through planning, or by putting people through a Harvard
> Business School class by one Highly Decorated Professor of Innovation
> and Entrepreneurship (who never innovated anything) or hiring a
> consultant (who never innovated anything). This is a fallacy – note
> the disproportionate contribution of uneducated technicians and
> entrepreneurs to various technological leaps, from the Industrial
> Revolution to the emergence of Silicon Valley..."

Maybe that's the reason we feel that so many government initiatives to
promote innovation fail. We want to create a comfortable environment to
foster innovation, when it will only arise from discomfort. 


Antifragility is that which wakes up and overreacts to compensate for
harm and stressors. But we have to be careful not to confuse noise with
signal. Picture a neurotic leader, who overreacts to a small mishap in
his business. Compare him with another, imperturbable leader, who has
that ability to stay calm in adverse situations. These people react
occasionally, if they see the need, and unlike the neurotic one they
rarely get angry, but when they do everybody notices and takes it very
seriously. Even-tempered people react to real information; neurotics
react mainly to noise. Noise is what we should ignore; signal is what
matters most.

This is what happens in companies and in government. They fill up with
people, even whole departments, specialized in overreacting to noise
while ignoring the signal. 

> "There is so much noise coming from the media's glorification of the
> anecdote. Thanks to this, we are living more and more in a virtual
> reality, separated from the real world, and we notice it less and
> less. Likewise, by presenting us with explanations and theories, the
> media induce an illusion of understanding the world. When constrained
> systems, those hungry for natural disorder, collapse, as they are
> eventually bound to, since they are fragile, failure is never seen as
> the result of fragility. Rather, such failure is interpreted as the
> product of poor forecasting (which, they say, needs to be
> "improved"). As with a crumbling sand pile, it would be unintelligent
> to attribute the collapse of a fragile bridge to the last truck that
> crossed it, and even more foolish to try to predict in advance which
> truck might bring it down. Yet it is done all too often. What should
> be studied is the system and its fragility, not the events. Let's not
> fool ourselves: the success rate in forecasting important rare events
> in fields like politics or economics is not close to zero; it is
> zero."

All living beings have a finite life. But these beings try to leave
offspring with a genetic code that differs in some respect from their
parents'. To nature, individuals aren't very useful once they have
exhausted their reproductive capacity; it lets the game go on at the
level of information, through the genetic code. In this way, organisms
have to die for nature, that cruel, opportunistic and selfish nature, to
be antifragile.\

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/d5e67668-6118-11e8-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7)

In the right context, stressors are information. For an antifragile
system the[ harm caused by errors should be less than the benefit.
Without the Titanic, we would have kept building bigger and bigger ocean
liners, and the tragedy of a shipwreck, which was inevitably going to
happen, might have been worse in number of dead. This shows the
difference between the benefits for the system and the harm to some of
its components or individuals. ]{style="letter-spacing: 0.01rem;"}

> "For the economy to be
> antifragile and undergo what we call evolution, each individual
> business must necessarily be fragile, exposed to going under; because,
> in order to improve, evolution needs organisms (or their genes) to die
> and be replaced by fitter ones, or the less fit not to reproduce." 
> \[\...\]
> "It is unpleasant to think that
> cruelty is an engine of improvement. But there are ways to mitigate
> the harm to the weakest. The solution is to create a system in which
> the fall of one cannot drag others down, because continuous failures
> work to preserve the system. Paradoxically, many social policies and
> government interventions end up hurting the weak and consolidating the
> established."

> "In order to progress, modern society should be treating ruined
> entrepreneurs in the same way we honor dead soldiers... For there is
> no such thing as a failed soldier, dead or alive (unless he acted in a
> cowardly manner)—likewise, there is no such thing as a failed
> entrepreneur or failed scientific researcher..."

The interesting thing about Taleb's analysis is that it tells us that
**"Fragility can be measured; risk is not measurable"**

So, if we think about security and that is what we're after, we have to
understand that there is something more important, which is our
survival. Our mission in life is to be antifragile. But to make progress
on this we have to understand the difference between true stability and
artificial stability:

> Consider the fate of the brothers John and George. John has spent
> twenty-five years as a clerk in the personnel department of a large
> bank. George is a taxi driver.
>
> John has a perfectly predictable salary (or so he thinks), plus
> health insurance and four weeks of vacation a year. He tends to wake
> up telling himself that "life is good". But that was before the
> banking crisis broke out and he realized his job would become
> "redundant". Being unemployed would hit him very hard **since he is
> over fifty.**
>
> George has good days when he can make good money; others are worse
> and he barely covers his costs; but he ends up making more or less
> the same as his brother. Given the variability of his income, he
> always complains about not enjoying his brother's job security; but,
> deep down, this longing is an illusion, because he will come out
> better off than him.
> 
> This is the central illusion in life: that randomness is risky, that
> it is a bad thing—and that eliminating randomness is done by
> eliminating randomness. The self-employed have an income with a
> certain volatility, but they are fairly robust to a minor Black
> Swan\[2\] that could leave them without income. Their risks are very
> clear. But the same is not true of salaried employees: they have no
> volatility and are taken by surprise when their income goes to
> nothing after being laid off. Their risks are hidden.

So we shouldn't concentrate on predicting future catastrophe scenarios;
what we should do is identify our fragilities, and perhaps expose
ourselves to stressors that put us to the test, to learn how to react to
external events that exploit our vulnerabilities.

There is a Yiddish proverb that says ***"Provide for the worst; the
best can take care of itself"***. It sounds like a truism, but it's very
important for understanding this antifragility business.
