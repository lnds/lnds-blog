When we work in a group we are always influencing one another, for
better or for worse. Together we can achieve things that seemed
impossible; other times we fail miserably. Either way, every group
exhibits what students of social behavior call ["process
loss"](https://psychologydictionary.org/process-loss/).

This concept is similar to the consequences of the laws of
thermodynamics. That is, just as it's impossible to have one hundred
percent efficiency in a physical system, the same goes for a group of
people: the group's performance is always less than the sum of the
individual performances. 

Working in a group carries a series of costs, which can come from
coordination problems or from loss of motivation.

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/336120d3-8e19-11e8-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7)

The figure[^1] shows this effect. The group's actual productivity is
less than the sum of the individual contributions, always.

If working in a team is inefficient, why do we do it?

The most obvious reason is that the tasks we take on as a group are
often too big for one individual. It's the way we have of dealing with
the complexity of today's projects.

The important thing is to remember that as we take on complex projects,
and form groups and hierarchies to tackle them, we have to pay a price,
and that price is *"process loss"*.

## Some causes

Apart from the losses inherent to coordination within a team, another
source of reduced productivity is loss of motivation.

One of the phenomena observed in teamwork is what is known as
[social loafing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_loafing)[^2]. This phenomenon
was first observed in a series of experiments carried out
by [Max Ringelmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Ringelmann),
who discovered the effect that bears his name.

The "Ringelmann effect", also known as "free rider behavior" or the
"free-rider problem", is a concept that names the tendency for
performance in a group to drop as the size of the group increases. 

Ringelmann ran a series of experiments with groups playing tug of war.
Effort was measured both for individuals and for groups of two, three
and so on up to eight people per team.

{{<figure caption="Results of Ringelmann's experiments" src="https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/bb8e0cf4-8e1b-11e8-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7">}}

When the group reached eight people, individuals were exerting less
than half the effort they would exert on their own.

{{<figure caption="We've all noticed the Ringelmann effect at some point" src="https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/362b1876-8e28-11e8-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7">}}

Ringelmann was the first to identify the effects of process loss;
according to him there are two kinds of problems[^3]:

> **Loss of motivation:** the reduction of individual effort exerted
> when people work in groups compared to when they work alone.
> According to Ringelmann, group members tend to rely on their
> co-workers to provide the effort needed for a common task.

> **Coordination problems:** When individuals come together in groups
> to perform a task, their performance depends on their individual
> resources and the various interpersonal processes at work within the
> group. Even if the group members have the skills and knowledge needed
> to complete an assigned task, they may fail to coordinate their
> efforts in a productive way.
>

In 1993, Karau and Williams[^4] conducted a meta-analysis of different
studies on this phenomenon and made some interesting findings, such as,
for example, that among women from Eastern cultures the social loafing
effect was reduced. After analyzing a number of results, these authors
proposed what they called the collective effort model:

```
Expectancy X Instrumentality X Valence of Outcome = Motivational Force
```

In this model, **Expectancy** is the degree to which high levels of
effort are expected to lead to similarly high levels of performance. 
**Instrumentality** is the degree to which high-quality performance is
perceived as instrumental in obtaining an outcome. **Valence of
Outcome** is the degree to which the outcome is perceived as desirable. 

However, the authors acknowledge that although individual and group
performance is strongly related to many of the valued outcomes, there
are some outcomes that don't depend on performance.

For example, when working on intrinsically meaningful tasks, or when
working with highly respected colleagues, exerting high levels of effort
can lead to greater self-satisfaction, approval from the group, or other
important outcomes, even when that high effort has little or no impact
on tangible performance results.

So there are ways to reduce the lack of motivation, and it depends on
the makeup of the team and its intrinsic motivations.


## How to reduce process loss

According to Dan J. Rothwell, there are three elements for reducing the
loss due to lack of motivation: Collaboration, Content and Choice (the
3 Cs).

**Collaboration** is a way of getting everyone involved by assigning
each member special, meaningful work. It's the way the members of the
group share knowledge and the tasks that absolutely must be completed.
If you have two people sitting idle because they weren't given specific
jobs, what you have to do is give them tasks that make them feel
essential to the group, because that way they won't want the team's
performance to drop, since they have specific obligations to fulfill.

**Content** identifies the importance of the specific tasks within the
group. If the members of the group see their role as necessary for
completing a worthwhile task, they are more likely to fulfill it. People
need to feel that meeting their obligations will be valued by the
group.

**Choice** is giving the members of the group the opportunity to pick
the task they want to carry out. Assigning roles in a group causes
complaints and frustration. Giving group members the freedom to choose
their role makes social loafing less significant, and motivates the
members of the group to work as a team.

This is one strategy; there are other proposals, and which one to pick
depends on each group and of course on the leader. But the important
thing is to understand that this effect exists; detecting it in time and
coming up with strategies to lessen its impact is one of the duties of a
good team leader. 

Personally I think that if you align reducing process loss with the
self-satisfaction of the team members, you'll get the best results.
