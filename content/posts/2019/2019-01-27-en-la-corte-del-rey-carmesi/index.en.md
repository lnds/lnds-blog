King Crimson was formed in London on November 30, 1968, and held its
first rehearsal on **January 14, 1969**, fifty years ago now. The band
was the heir to Giles, Giles and Fripp, a group that played pop with an
eccentric style and high musical complexity. Originally they were a
trio made up of the brothers Michael Giles on drums and Peter Giles on
bass, together with guitarist Robert Fripp. In time they wanted to
expand their sound and brought in Ian McDonald on keyboards, who in
turn brought in Peter Sinfield as lyricist.

Fripp wanted to leave pop behind and pushed for Peter Giles to quit the
band, and he invited Greg Lake, an old friend and former schoolmate.
Since both played guitar (they had studied with the same teacher), and
after Giles's departure, they decided Lake would play bass and take
over the vocals.

The band's name is a reference to Beelzebub, prince of demons\[1\]. But
also to the fact that a "crimson king" historically refers to a king
whose reign is marked by excessive bloodshed. Remember that the band's
first album came out right in the middle of the Vietnam War.

The main composer was McDonald, with contributions from Fripp, and the
lyrics were Sinfield's. Fripp once said that adding lyrics to King
Crimson's music was "a functional impossibility". They worked
intensely, searching for new sounds and complicated harmonies, until
in October 1969 they released "In The Court of the Crimson King".

{{<figure src="https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/3e6deba1-2280-11e9-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7" caption="Cover image of King Crimson's first album; the painting is the work of Barry Godber, a computer programmer.">}}

The album was a success, and the band went on tour, mainly across the
United States. But that trip opened cracks in the band. Michael Giles
and Ian McDonald quit the group in the middle of the tour. Back home,
and while they were preparing their second album, Greg Lake decided to
leave as well, to form the famous progressive rock supertrio "Emerson,
Lake & Palmer".

Fripp invited Gordon Haskell, another former schoolmate, to join the
band, replacing Lake. By then Sinfield was in charge of the
synthesizers.

It wasn't easy for Haskell to take over Lake's vocal parts, and he
even asked to lower the key of some songs so he could sing them
properly. The proposal was vigorously rejected by Fripp and the two
grew apart. Later Haskell would say *"The King Crimson weapon is
musical fascism, made by fascists, designed by fascists to dehumanize,
to strip mankind of his dignity and soul." *\[2\].

Eventually, and after recording "Islands", the band's fourth album,
Fripp asked Sinfield to leave the group. With that, Robert Fripp
became the brain, heart and soul of King Crimson.

Despite always having the backing of top-quality musicians, Fripp
systematically refused to include their compositions, with the idea of
keeping control over quality and making sure King Crimson played "the
right kind of music".

**Haskell**
-----------

> "Haskell is a compiler that transforms insecure narcissism into
> intellectual superiority" - [\@progmofo on
> Twitter](https://twitter.com/progmofo/status/1080158952816553984)

Of the programming languages I know**, I can say that
[Haskell](https://www.haskell.org/) is one of the hardest to master.
Writing a program in Haskell is probably like rehearsing in a session
with Robert Fripp. But it is worth it, just like listening to King
Crimson's music, I assure you.

But if you don't challenge your tastes, your conventions, and
everything you have learned, how are you going to learn? A colleague
once said, "functional programming is hard, but like everything
worthwhile in life, it is hard".

In 1987, at the conference on Functional Programming Languages and
Computer Architecture, a committee was formed to define an open
standard to consolidate all the functional languages that existed at
the time (dozens of them).

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/6ac3e823-2281-11e9-a030-2b5831f8ecb5-aa9f18b7)

The name was chosen to honor Haskell Curry, an American mathematician
and logician who worked on combinatory logic, which serves as the
foundation for a style of functional programming.

The first version of Haskell was published in 1990 and the work
continued until the release of Haskell 98, the first standard. With
that definition a team at the University of Glasgow worked on the
implementation of GHC ("The Glorious Glasgow Haskell Compilation
System"). The work was started by Kevin Hammond, and it is currently
led by Simon Peyton Jones and Simon Marlow.

Marlow has worked at Facebook since 2013, and there he has used
Haskell to implement and reimplement a series of tools in that
language. A notable example is the Sigma rule engine, which applies
security policies to every interaction that takes place when someone
posts something on the social network \[3\].

## **Heroes**

Fripp disbanded King Crimson in 1974 after releasing the album Red.
After that he worked on various projects, as a session musician and
also as a solo artist. He collaborated with Brian Eno, David Bowie,
David Byrne and Peter Gabriel among others, who admired Fripp's style
on guitar.

Perhaps the best known thing from this period, for the general public,
is his part on David Bowie's 1977 album Heroes, where his guitar stands
out on the song that gives the record its name (my favorite in all of
Bowie's work). Heroes is a song representative of what we now call
art rock. It is inspired by the story of a pair of lovers separated by
the Berlin Wall.

{{<youtube 2JywkrIiXW8>}}

In 1981 Fripp decided to re-form King Crimson, calling back Bill
Bruford, the former Yes drummer, with whom he had worked on the album
Red. Bruford was joined by the great bassist Tony Levin and by Adrian
Belew (whom Fripp met when they were playing for Peter Gabriel). Belew
was a singer and guitarist who had worked with Bowie, Talking Heads
and Frank Zappa (with whom he started his career).

Originally the band formed by Fripp, Bruford, Levin and Belew was
called Discipline, but the members felt it was more fitting to
associate it with King Crimson. For Fripp, the name King Crimson has
been associated with a "way of doing things", more than with a
particular kind of group and musicians.

## **Discipline**

The first album by this new King Crimson lineup was called Discipline.
For four years they worked together and presented their work in three
albums: Discipline, Beat, Three of a Perfect Pair. This is perhaps one
of the King Crimson lineups most valued by its fans. This time Fripp
opened up to his bandmates' contributions and they worked together on

### **No side effects**

If there is one thing that characterizes Haskell as a programming
language, it is its strict discipline with respect to types, and its
adherence to the functional principles of immutability and the absence
of side effects.

No useful program can be built without side effects. For example,
printing a simple message on the screen means affecting the
environment. To comply with its strict discipline, Haskell resorts to a
concept that makes it possible to isolate these side effects, a concept
that comes from Category Theory and is called a Monad.

Let's look at the classic example:

```haskell
     Module Main (main) where
     main:: IO()
     main = putStrLen “Hola Mundo”
```

In this case, by declaring the function main we state that its result
is an IO Monad, that is, a data type that isolates a side effect. That
is why the compiler lets us use the impure function putStrLen\[4\].

It is this strict discipline that intimidates many programmers, in the
same way that some musicians felt intimidated by Fripp's perfectionist
leadership.

### **Huffman coding in Haskell**

Despite that strict discipline, in Haskell we can write very concise
programs. Solving challenge 4 took just 91 lines of code.

We are going to analyze the compression process in this article; the
complete source code is in the repository on
[GitHub](https://github.com/lnds/9d9l/tree/master/desafio4/haskell).

The function **compress** is the one that receives as parameters the
name of the file we want to compress (input) and leaves the result in a
file whose name comes in the second parameter:

```haskell
     compress input output = do
       src <- LB.unpack <$> LB.readFile input
       let tree = buildTree $ calcFreq src
       let table = M.fromList $ serializeTree tree
       let lookup chr = fromJust $ M.lookup chr table
       let binaryOut = concat $ map lookup src
       let treeOut = binarizeTree tree
       let outputStream = padRight8 (treeOut ++ binaryOut)
       LB.writeFile output $ LB.pack $ map fromBits (chunksOf 8 outputStream)
```

This function has side effects, and that follows from the fact that it
uses the do statement, which in Haskell always indicates that we will
produce a side effect.

The first thing is to get all the bytes of the file, which is achieved
with:

```haskell
     src <- LB.unpack <$> LB.readFile input
```

In this case we use LB, which is declared at the beginning of the
program like this:

```haskell
     import qualified Data.ByteString.Lazy.Char8 as LB
```

LB has everything needed to read and write files of bytes.

LB.unpack unpacks a string into a sequence of bytes, LB.readFile reads
the entire contents of a file as a string.

The \<\$\> operator is identical to calling the function fmap, and in
this case what it does is map the string to a list of bytes.

Next we must build the tree, which is done with:

```haskell
    let tree = buildTree $ calcFreq src
```

You will notice that on the first line, to assign to src, I used the
\<- operator, but to assign to tree we use let tree =. The reason is
that to pull out the values of an IO Monad you use \<-, but with pure
functions you use let, which is the standard in Haskell. As for the \$
sign, it is there to avoid ambiguities and is explained in note \[4\].

The trick is in the function calcFreq, which I present below:

```haskell
     calcFreq = ((length &&& head) <$>) . group . sort
```

The dot operator (.) denotes function composition.

In Haskell (f . g) x is equivalent to writing f (g x).

So calcFreq is a function that returns a new function composed of the
application of several functions.

This must be read from right to left to be understood.

The first function is sort, which sorts the input.

The second function is group, which groups the sorted elements.

Suppose our file contains the phrase:

     cat's foot iron claw

Then sort produces:

      aaccfilnooosttw'

Then group produces:

      (aa)(cc)(f)(I)(n)(ooo)(s)(tt)(w)

(length &&& head) is what is technically known as an Arrow; the &&&
operator applies the two functions it receives as parameters and
generates a pair with the values obtained, so

```haskell
    (length &&& head) (ooo) => (3, o)
```

The function length computes the length of a list, and head gets its
first element.

By doing \<\$\>, we map each element with the Arrow
(length &&& head).

That is, by doing ((length &&& head) \<\$\>) over
(aa)(cc)(f)(I)(n)(ooo)(s)(tt)(w) we get:

      ((2, a), (2,c),(1,f),(1,l),(1,n),(3,o),(1,s),(2,t),(1,w))

The function buildTree is the following:

```haskell
     buildTree = htree . S.fromList . map (second Leaf)
```

To understand what this function does we must introduce the data type
HTree, which is declared like this:

```haskell
     data HTree a = Leaf a | Branch (HTree a) (HTree a)
```

So let's explain what buildTree does.

What map (second Leaf) does is turn every second element of the input
pairs into leaves:

     ((2, a), (2,c),(1,f),(1,l),(1,n),(3,o),(1,s),(2,t),(1,w))

Ending up like this:

     ((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c),(1, Leaf f),(1, Leaf l),(1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))

The function [S.fr](http://S.fr)omList simply converts this list into a
set, which makes it possible to operate with the function htree.

The function htree is defined like this:

```haskell
     htree ts
       | S.null ts_1 = t1
       | otherwise = htree ts_3
       where
        ((w1, t1), ts_1) = S.deleteFindMin ts
        ((w2, t2), ts2) = S.deleteFindMin ts_1
        ts3 = S.insert (w1 + w2, Branch t1 t2) ts_2
```

Here we have implemented Huffman's algorithm. First you have to
understand what the expressions that come after the where statement do.

Remember that Haskell does pattern matching, so we can see that:

```haskell
     ((w1, t1), ts_1) = S.deleteFindMin ts
```

Extracts the node with the minimum value from the set. In our example
this would work out like this:

```haskell
     ((w1, t1), ts_1) = S.deleteFindMin((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c),(1, Leaf f),(1, Leaf l),(1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
     =>
     ((w1, t1), ts_1) = ((1, Leaf f), (2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf l),(1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
     =>
     (w1=1, t1=Leaf f)
     ts_1 = ((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf l),(1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
```

The next expression is:

```haskell
      ((w2, t2), ts_2) = S.deleteFindMin ts_1
```

Which in our example would result in:

```haskell
       ((w2, t2), ts_2) = S.deleteFindMin    ((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf l),(1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
     =>
     ((w2, t2), ts_2) = ((1, Leaf l),  ((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w)))
     => (w2 = 1, t2 = Leaf l)
     ts_2 =  ((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
```

The third expression is:

```haskell
     ts_3 = S.insert (w1 + w2, Branch t1 t2) ts_2
```

Which, in the example we are looking at, would result in:

```haskell
           ts_3 = S.insert (1+ 1, Branch (Leaf f) (Leaf l))   ((2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
     => ts_3 = ((2, Branch (Leaf f) (Leaf l)),     (2, Leaf a), (2, Leaf c), (1, Leaf n),(3, Leaf o),(1,Leaf s),(2, Leaf t),(1, Leaf w))
```

Given these expressions, we can understand the body of the function
htree:

```haskell
     htree ts
     | S.null ts_1 = t1
     | otherwise = htree ts_3 
```

This is a recursive function. What it tells us is that if the set it
receives is empty then it takes the element t1 (which corresponds to
the leaf with the minimum value). Otherwise it recursively applies
htree to the rest of the set.

By now, if you have made it this far, you must hate me as much as
Gordon Haskell hated Robert Fripp when he left King Crimson, but I ask
you for a little more patience.

What we do with this strange function is simply a loop, which builds
the tree from the list of frequencies, so that the minimum value always
ends up at the root. To understand it, I suggest you expand the example
all the way to the end.

With this we have built our Huffman tree.

Let's go back to the function compress. The next expression is:

```haskell
    let table = M.fromList $ serializeTree tree
```

I am not going to explain serializeTree; suffice it to say that it
converts the tree into a table of pairs in which the first value is the
character to compress, and the second element of the pair is its
representation as a list of zeros and ones.

That way we get the conversion table.

To be able to apply it we will use a helper function, which is the one
defined in the following expression:

```haskell
     let lookup chr = fromJust $ M.lookup chr table
```

The function lookup receives a character and looks it up in the table.
The function fromJust is needed because M.lookup returns a structure of
type Just. This is a standard construct in Haskell for knowing whether
or not an element is in a dictionary.

With this we can generate the compressed output, expressed as a string
of bits:

```haskell
     let binaryOut = concat $ map lookup src
```

What this does is map each character to a list of bits, then join all
the lists into a single one, called binaryOut.

We must also map the tree to a sequence of bits, and for that we call
the function binarizeTree

```haskell
     let treeOut = binarizeTree tree
```

With this we can assemble our output, which is one big list of bits:

```haskell
     let outputStream = padRight8 (treeOut ++ binaryOut)
```

It could happen that our output is something like this

     [1,0,1,1,0,0,0,1,0,1]

With 10 values, but we need the size of the output to be a multiple of
8, since bytes are made of eight. What the function padRigh8 does is
add zeros on the right until the length is a multiple of 8:

```haskell
     [1,0,1,1,0,0,0,1,0,1,0,0,0,0,0,0]
```

This is necessary because when we finally write the result to a file
we do:

```haskell
     LB.writeFile output $ LB.pack $ map fromBits (chunksOf 8 outputStream)
```

The function chunksOf groups the output into batches of 8 bits.
