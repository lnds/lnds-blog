> *Come as you are, as you were\
> -- Kurt Cobain*

I remember the day Kurt Cobain died. I heard about his death on the
radio; I was with some friends and partners from the company where I
worked.

It was April 1994, and we were working on a project for the Chilean
internal revenue service. I liked Nirvana, the whole grunge scene in
general, Alice in Chains, Pearl Jam, etc. That afternoon the news hit
us hard, because Cobain was not only an independent rock star, he was
also a young guy about our age.

On the other hand, in a way I felt that we, in our little software
development company, were an indie rock band too.

I don't know whether I can talk about the system we were building back
then; I will only say that my partners and I implemented a technology
that was quite innovative for the time. In many ways it was a grunge
solution.

Grunge seems simple, in musical terms. Its stance was to get away from
the style of the more commercial rock and pop. The idea was to go back
to basics. The amp cranked way up, the drums played very fast and very
hard, along with a singer with a ragged voice, almost screaming. And of
course depressing, disenchanted lyrics, with a bit of protest against
the prevailing social alienation. Grunge takes a lot from punk, and
early heavy metal, from Neil Young's protest rock, or from David
Bowie's first melancholy songs.

Back then we listened to good music while we worked. We took turns
picking tracks; we had a computer with a CD drive and some good
speakers, so it could be heard all over the office. We listened to
almost everything, but mainly rock. Our customers were companies that
provided support services to the banks, or a few government agencies;
we were also subcontractors for bigger companies.

Above all we wanted our independence. Our guitars and drums were
algorithms written in C, or C++. We used Linux when almost none of our
customers knew it existed. It's not that we were particularly
idealistic about open source; the truth is it was a more pragmatic
position. It was cheaper for us than installing SCO, Solaris or AIX.
It was only when we landed an important contract with a telco that we
were able to buy a Sun SPARC, and there we installed Oracle. Our PCs
had DOS, Windows for Workgroups and Linux (over time I started using
Yggdrasil, but it is almost certain we installed
[Slackware](http://www.slackware.com/), for the geeks interested in
those details).

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/91213a19-4917-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)

That solution we were working on in April 1994, for the internal
revenue service, was odd by that institution's standards. They were
only just migrating to Oracle at the time and had an enormous amount of
legacy code in Cobol. Since our process was written in C and used no
database at all, it was hard to get them to adopt it. We even wrote a
small domain-specific language that mimicked Cobol syntax, to ease
adoption. But even so there was reluctance to use it afterwards. They
never called us again. After all, it was more reliable to use Oracle
than that grunge solution written by a few hackers barely surviving in
a small company.

But we delivered our solution on time, and it worked very well, too
well. I suspect they had never seen anything solve that particular
problem so fast.

We did things like that at Enfasis, which was the name of this small
company. I don't remember whether I was a partner in 1994, but in time
they invited me to become one.

I remember that we once developed an [SLR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_LR_parser) compiler that translated
[EDI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_data_interchange) documents into XML,
or HTML. We also designed cryptographic solutions for banking and for
other customers I'm not sure I can mention (I don't want men in black
knocking on my door). We also implemented many SCADA projects; I have
[written a bit about that before](/en/blog/lnds/2010/08/21/debugging-stories/).

One of the great things about that small company was that every design
was discussed by all the partners. I remember [Marco and Ricardo](/en/blog/lnds/2008/10/05/the-hackers-of-october-5/) with their cigarettes, asking hard questions and arguing with each other.
Or Rodrigo, who made his case in front of the screen, writing code.
Jorge, who preferred to show a proof of concept. They all looked at me
funny when I wrote the proposals to customers in LaTeX, and wrote my
code using
[CTangle](http://www.literateprogramming.com/cweb_download.html) and
[CWEB](http://www.literateprogramming.com/cweb_download.html).
We were young, smart, daring and innovative.

Some five or six years later, in 1999 or 2000, Ricardo and I developed
a piece of software for the Chamber of Commerce that cut a process
which took a whole night down to minutes, software that was still in
use in 2015, as I was able to confirm (I suspect it is still in
production).

I am proud of that time, because we built solutions that were well
thought out, validated by many heads, tested in theory and in
practice.

It was at that company that I started experimenting with [Extreme Programming](https://www.extremeprogramming.org). Every so often we
gave internal presentations on some technology. I remember submitting
a proposal (probably in late '97 or in '98) to a customer who required
us to describe our working methodology, or propose one. In that
proposal I included a diagram like this one:

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/e911eeda-4918-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)

When I read about XP, I realized we were doing a lot of XP without
knowing it. So I explained to the team what it consisted of and we
tried to implement it.

But innovation and individual intelligence and mastery are not enough,
and they guarantee nothing. We had several problems with sales
management and project management. Maybe we were too innovative, on
top of being perfectionists. We also oversold ourselves many times. We
lacked the experience to run a software development company. Not
everything is about individual technical skills.

We made money, but there were bills to pay, employees' salaries, and
we partners were not satisfied with our income. I was also in debt, my
first marriage had failed, and I wasn't excited about what I was
doing.

Jorge, one of my former customers, became operations manager at the
Chamber of Commerce and invited me to join his team. The grunge days
were over\...
