Wake Up!
--------

> I'll give you a dose but it'll never come close to the\
> Rage built up inside of me\
> --- Rage Against the Machine

In April 2003 I was out of work, and broke, because I had gone several
months without a paycheck. I was in anguish, my body and my mind weren't
responding. Exhausted and worn out.

Leaving the CCS, the Santiago Chamber of Commerce, had been a complete
failure. I was *"keeping the pot on the stove"*, as we say in Chile,
with odd jobs landed through contacts. The story of how I got there
still makes me angry, because I was deceived, swindled like many
citizens, like the Chilean state itself.

After leaving the Chamber, I went to work at a startup, created by
someone I met through CEO. He ran a successful web design agency and
had several important contracts, most of them with the government. His
offices were in a beautiful old mansion in Providencia, where some of
the lunches with entrepreneurs that I told you about in [part 5](/en/blog/lnds/2019/03/31/the-end-of-agile-v/) were sometimes held.

When I started working there the startup hadn't been incorporated yet.
So I worked with the agency's team, replacing the head of technology,
who had left. That's where I met Rafa, who was the company's developer.
The rest were account managers and a couple of graphic designers. While
the *due diligence* was being done to receive the seed capital that
would give rise to the startup, I took the chance to get to know their
technology, and I helped out on some projects of the parent company.

## Cult of Personality

> Look in my eyes, what do you see?\
> The cult of personality\
> --- Living Colours

I remember that one of the partners had a modeling agency at the same
address. It wasn't unusual to see beautiful girls on the second floor
running around in their underwear to change outfits during photo
shoots, putting together their books. Several models passed through
there who were starting to get famous right then, for appearing on the
fledgling programs called reality shows,  and other celebrity gossip
shows that invaded the television of the time.

I should have suspected something right then. The atmosphere was very
strange. Rafa and I worked at the back of the house. One day a German
kid, about 22 years old, showed up asking for a job. He could design,
program in Python, and knew a strange platform called
[Zope](http://www.zope.org/en/latest/)[.
I took a look at it, and it was a very interesting web content server.
We hired him, because the agency's technical area had to be left well
staffed before Rafa and I moved over.

I have to say that at least during that time they kept their
commitment to pay me what we'd agreed. But I was worried, because that
wasn't what I had signed up for. I was supposed to be developing
software for a platform of interactive mobile games.

## Bulls on parade

> The microphone explodes, shattering the molds\
> [--- Rage Against the Machine]{style="letter-spacing: 0.01rem;"}

When [Tom Morello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Morello) was
[asked on Twitter](https://twitter.com/tmorello/status/894357738482016257) which city has the
best fans, the musician answered, Rock: Santiago, Baseball: Chicago.


![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/6aceb776-5879-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)


For Rage Against the Machine, one of their best concerts was the one
that became known as the [Battle of Santiago](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lhZA6ifWbQo), in 2010.
Their style, which mixes funk and rap with metal, with political lyrics,
makes them one of the greatest exponents of rap metal and funk metal.

The use of Wake Up in the soundtrack of The Matrix made a big
impression on me. I think it burned that movie's message into my mind.
It's the right closing note. Too bad they never made a second or third
part of The Matrix\[1\].

They say RATM's music was used by the CIA to torture prisoners at
Guantanamo \[2\]. The idea was to wreck their nerves by playing this
aggressive music at full volume. It's a terrible irony and proof that
the system can absorb anything, even what goes against it, and use it
to its own advantage.

For me funk metal is tied to violent energy, to that pent-up rage I
kept accumulating through 2002 and 2003, which blew up into severe
stomach pains and depression.

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/2a5ddabc-5881-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)

## Evidence

> Anything you say, we know you're guilty\
> --- Faith no more

Suddenly some executives walked into this comedy, with experience in
retail and television. They would be the startup's commercial and
executive arm.

These days, in early 2019, the founders of several successful startups,
like Cornershop and NotCo, are sharing their view of how a startup
should be built, through [a class at the Universidad de Chile](http://www.laclase.cl/)[, whose content is being made
public.

If you watch [the first video](https://youtu.be/vUePmkQYIDQ) of this
course you'll get one of these founders' take on how to build a
successful startup. It's advice drawn from his own experience, which at
least worked for him and his partners. Well, the people at the startup
I was in did exactly the opposite.

First they put together a pompous board: there was a former minister, a
former television director, retail managers and of course a
representative of the venture capitalist.

Technology was seen as secondary. They paid us well and gave us money
to buy equipment, but there were just two of us. I was barely asked
about an IT or development plan. As far as they were concerned the
platform was already done, and it was just a matter of connecting it to
the carriers' networks.

It's about time I told you what the hell the business was about.

[At one CEO meeting we had Carlos Baradello as a guest. Today this
gentleman is a founder and manager of an important venture capital fund
in Sausalito, California. But in those years he was in charge of an
important area at Motorola. He visited Chile because that company wanted
to set up an R&D center in our country. It was at that lunch that
several of us realized the future was what back then was called "the
mobile web".

In Europe, as a result of the reality show craze and the SMS boom,
there were already experiments with interactive television.
[Big Brother](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Brother_(programa_de_televisi%C3%B3n)),
for example, was a reality show where the audience could vote by
sending messages from their cell phones.

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/bb71381d-5883-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)


In 2002, these guys from the agency built a prototype for a trivia game
to be used during the soccer World Cup. That's how they got the idea of
setting up a mobile games startup using SMS. But the engineer who had
developed the platform together with Rafa had walked out on them. So
they approached me at a lunch and offered me the job of CTO of this new
startup.

I accepted, after my failed negotiation at the CCS. And so suddenly, in
late 2002, I was setting up equipment on a spacious floor of a building
in Vitacura. It was a very elegant office. We bought high-end laptops.
We had a fancy coffee machine, an efficient and friendly assistant,
comfortable leather chairs, and dozens of cell phones to test with.

The only bad thing, the only evidence that this was headed down the
wrong road, though I didn't know it, was that the investor was called
[Inverlink Holding](https://www.emol.com/especiales/corrupcion/inverlink/origen.htm).


![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/c8ae594e-5883-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)


## Don Ricardo's vase

> Can you feel it, see it, hear it today?\
> If you can't, then it doesn't matter anyway\
> You will never understand it 'cause it happens too fast\
> -- Faith no more

We set up shop in September 2002, more or less. I signed a new
contract, this time with the startup, with the title of technology
manager.

But in November the paychecks came late, and the same in December and
January. In February we got nothing at all. Meanwhile, in that period
we didn't land a single meaningful deal. Despite being connected to
every cell phone carrier, and the pompous board meetings full of
dignitaries, and our executives' résumés, we weren't managing to sell
much.

It seemed the partners cared more about getting to the next investment
rounds than about selling anything. I'll say it again: everything you
shouldn't do was being done at this company.

In February 2003 it came out that the secretary of Carlos Massad, head
of the Central Bank, had been sending emails with inside information to
Enzo Bertinelli, with whom she was having an affair. That set off an
investigation that uncovered one of the biggest frauds against the
public treasury in our history. Inverlink had committed several crimes,
and among other things it was illegally obtaining funds from Corfo, the
state development agency, with which it financed, among other things,
tech startups like the one I was in. Guess who was one of the directors
of our startup. Yes, Enzo Bertinelli himself.

It was at that time, when the scandal broke at CORFO, which touched his
son-in-law, that President Lagos delivered his famous vase theory:

> "Not one peso of the Chilean State has been lost here. If you have a
> vase in your house and a thief breaks in and steals it, when they find
> the vase they are going to give it back to you"

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/b2693c79-5880-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)


The problem is that the vase was broken. And I was among the pieces.

I didn't even get the chance to quit. One day we showed up for work and
found the door shut, sealed, with chains and padlocks. The secretary,
Rafa and I looked at each other. There was nobody else. We went for a
coffee, said our goodbyes, wished each other luck and went home. Just
like so many other employees of the Inverlink companies, which ranged
from small tech startups, through insurance and investment firms, to a
major hospital. More than three thousand of us were left without a job
because of the stolen vase, and we were never compensated. \[3\]


## Reboot

> Can't stop, addicted to the shindig --- Red Hot Chili Peppers

Remember the young German we had hired? One Friday he came to visit us
at our Vitacura offices. He had stayed on in charge of maintaining the
agency's sites. We talked a lot. He was a pleasant, intelligent kid. He
had come to Chile for love, and he told us he was planning to go back
and resume his studies in Germany in a few months. We said goodbye that
Friday. On Monday I was told he was in the hospital in a coma. On
Tuesday he died of a stroke. He had a tumor that had never been
diagnosed. The news hit me hard. It was devastating, I remember. It
made me very sad.

Since I had no job, I survived by taking part in a few freelance
projects at friends' companies.

In April of that year Jorge, who had served as the startup's general
manager, came to me to say there was a company interested in developing
mobile technology for logistics. I got along well with him, because in
the end he was the only one who faced us during the crisis, and he
tried to  help us. Besides, I respected him for his great intelligence
and honesty.

This time the funding came from a major  Chilean shipping company.
That's how a company was set up that they called Inphone. A curious and
prophetic name. It was a small startup that would use SMS messages to
coordinate routes, track fleets, and do anything else we could come up
with using SMS and the data technologies over GSM and GPRS, as long as
it made sense for the world of shipping logistics.

But for some reason, a rather clumsy one in my opinion, they also
decided to dabble in mobile marketing at the start. I felt that would
make us lose focus, but they argued it was something that could bring
in revenue quickly. They offered me a small ownership stake. The good
part was that this company, being part of the shipping company's
holding, let us receive the same benefits as its employees and
executives. I don't think I've ever had better benefits since.

Somehow the owner of the failed startup found out about all this, and
that's when I got a call from him. Perhaps the most unpleasant phone
conversation of my life.

He had worked himself into the idea that I was stealing his technology.
He insulted me and I told him where to go. We never saw each other
again until several years later, and on that occasion we didn't even
say hello. I suppose he still thinks I stole his technology, a
technology he never programmed, of course.

But in any case that's not what happened, for two very simple reasons.
First, I had no access to anything, because my laptop and the servers
were trapped behind those chains in that Vitacura office. The other
reason is that I never liked their technology, built in Perl with
Apache. They didn't even have an ESME\[3\]. If they managed to connect
to the mobile carriers it was because I was able to build a gateway
that let their application talk to the phone networks.

## Ships and fjords

> Just one note\
> Could make me float\
> Could make me float away\
> --- Red Hot Chili Peppers

At Inphone I built all the technology that was developed, because I was
the entire IT department.

[First I put together a digital marketing platform in Java. I also
wrote a pretty decent ESME in Java, based on an open source
project.]{style="letter-spacing: 0.01rem;"}\

But the most fun thing I did was working with some Inmarsat GPS units,
which were used on the ships that sailed the fjords of southern Chile.

![](https://d2dspjyoh5c79p.cloudfront.net/db9edb9a-5880-11e9-8e69-21c4c306beae-aa9f18b7)


These GPS units had a 64-byte buffer that could be used to transmit
messages. I built an application that made it possible to send
pre-coded messages (things like: *"entering port"*, *"cargo
delivered"*, *"held up by bad weather"*, etc.). We reserved 4 bytes for
the pre-coded messages (that gives a potential of more than 4 billion
possible messages, which I think was more than enough). The other 60
bytes were used to send a short text.

The ship's captain had a laptop connected to the Inmarsat GPS through
the serial port. In the user interface, a Windows application written
in Delphi, he selected the pre-coded message. Then he added some text
if necessary and pressed a button to dispatch the message.

In Valparaiso a server periodically polled Inmarsat's servers and
downloaded the messages from the ships, which arrived as files over
FTP. They were decoded and placed on a dashboard, also in Delphi. The
central operator could send instructions back to the ship, coded the
same way, using those 4 bytes.

The coding of messages and instructions was configured by an operator,
and if information needed to be added he had to distribute the message
table to every ship.

All this was done because in some fjords there was no radio signal and
the GPS was the only means of communication.

It was one of the most fun projects I've ever been part of, and one of
the things that satisfies me most is having built it all from scratch.
It required low-level programming, to handle the serial port and the
communications protocol with the GPS, something I had already done
during my time at Enfasis. It had a bit of mobile messaging, and I also
built some dashboards to report the ships' status and metrics.

Would you believe I put all that together in less than a month?

When you're focused and working alone, when every decision is yours,
the code can flow very fast, and on top of that you can surprise your
client by exceeding their expectations. And this matters a lot. Small
teams, even a single person, can be very efficient. What matters is
that the people are talented, are clear about what they want to do and
have the space and time to focus. That is essential to achieving true
agile development.

I don't know how long that solution stayed in use. Not long, I suppose,
since satellite communications technology developed very fast. But even
so, I think it's one of the most ingenious solutions I've ever
programmed.

But despite the benefits of working for the shipping company, I was
alone in that job. I'd go days without a single colleague in our little
office near the Tobalaba metro station.

There were barely three of us in the company. The manager, a sales
lead, and me, doing all the engineering.

I wasn't satisfied. On the contrary, I felt very depressed. I wanted to
emigrate, do new things and work on a team with colleagues who
understood me.

That's when I got the call from my friend Marco, in early 2004,
inviting me to venture into the world of biometrics. I'll tell you
about that in part seven.
