In the United States, taxis offer in their payment system the option of
leaving a tip of 15%, 20% or 25%. I don't have confirmation, but I
suspect that with the introduction of this system the average tip has
gone up to 20%. The reason this happens is a phenomenon that has to do
with what we talked about in the [previous article](/en/blog/lnds/2011/06/29/defaults/).

At the end of [Dan Ariely's video](https://www.ted.com/talks/lang/eng/dan_ariely_asks_are_we_in_control_of_our_own_decisions.html) we
are shown several cases of this manipulation of preferences. This kind
of manipulation can happen when there is asymmetric dominance of
attributes. Let's look at an example. Suppose we have to choose between
2 products. Product A is a high-quality but expensive camera. Product B
is a low-quality but cheaper camera. Under these conditions the decision
is hard. But if we introduce a third camera, C1, that is clearly more
expensive than camera A, then the decision gets easier, and people tend
to choose camera A. On the other hand, if a third camera C1 is
introduced that has the same price as B but lower quality, then people
tend to choose camera B. The diagram below shows this situation:

![](/images/2011/07/asimetria-atributos1.png)

The strange thing is that in the experiments carried out, the third
option introduced is never the one people choose. This phenomenon is
known as anchoring. In one experiment, for example, people taking part
in a wine auction were asked to write down the last two digits of their
Social Security number. Even though these numbers were random, and had
nothing to do with the auction that followed, it turned out that the
higher the number written down, the higher the bid those people were
willing to make. This is the phenomenon exploited by the American taxis
that use this payment system, since they get people to pay a 20% tip
almost every time.

We are irrational when it comes to choosing, and very easy to
manipulate. It has been found that human judgments are affected most
readily by the ease with which information is accessed or recalled.
People are more willing to believe a written statement if the typeface
is easier to read, or if they have read it before, even if it was
explicitly marked as false. In general, people tend to digest new
information in a way that reinforces what they already think. We tend to
notice the information that confirms our own beliefs more than the
information that doesn't. This is known as **confirmation bias**. On the
other hand, we are more subjective with information that goes against
our beliefs, and we are more skeptical of it and tend to subject it to
greater scrutiny. This is called **motivated reasoning**.

If you believe the dams in Patagonia are a bad thing, you will tend to
give more credit to the arguments that support that view, and you will
doubt and be skeptical of any argument against it, no matter how
objective it tries to be. The same goes for the opposite position.

Both confirmation bias and motivated reasoning largely prevent us from
resolving disputes, because the parties look at the same set of "facts"
and draw different impressions of reality from them. This happens even
in science, where scientists tend to question the evidence that
contradicts their preexisting beliefs. On this, Max Planck remarked:

> "A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its
> opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its
> opponents eventually die"

So there is a large number of relevant factors that affect our behavior,
and our decisions, in a really tangible and probably unsuspected way.
Psychologists have discovered many of these, such as
[priming](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primado_(psicolog%C3%ADa)),
[framing](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encuadre_(ciencias_sociales)),
[anchoring](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anclaje), the availability
heuristic, and several other forms of [cognitive
biases](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesgo_cognitivo). We are
irrational, and that is something we have to accept. Knowing this can
help us understand our peers better, and it can even help us in our
work. Like everything, this knowledge has two possible uses: it can be
used to manipulate behavior, or it can be used to notice when we are
being manipulated. But that decision is yours.
