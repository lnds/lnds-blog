There is a kind of logical fallacy called post hoc, from the Latin "post
hoc ergo propter hoc" ("after this, therefore because of this"), which
is a problem of false causality. It stems from the principle of
causality, that every effect must have a cause, and that the cause
precedes the effect: "since the rooster crows before dawn every day, the
rooster makes the sun come up".

Now, sampling bias, like the one we went over in
[our previous article](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/20/necessary-conditions/),
suffers from this kind of fallacy. If we see the wind blow and then the
branches and leaves of the trees move, it is safe to conclude that the
wind caused that movement; that reasoning seems safe. The problem is
that not every relation of precedence between events in time determines
causality. In physical phenomena the real causes can eventually be
found, because experiments can be repeated, but in social theories, or
even in theological ramblings, falling into the post hoc fallacy is very
common.

For example, Malcolm Gladwell's theory, the law of the few, falls into
the post hoc fallacy. Whenever an event happens such as a book that
becomes a bestseller, a big movie that breaks the box office, an artist
who suddenly becomes famous, or a product that turns into a hit, there
is someone who starts buying or doing something related to that event
before everyone else, and that person will look influential. Watts
criticizes this view of the influential few who generate these social
epidemics, since there is a series of other factors that trigger this
accidental influence.

The problem is that the post hoc fallacy can produce these accidental
influencers. Consider, for example, the epidemic of
[SRAS](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRAS), Severe Acute Respiratory
Syndrome, or SARS as it is known in English. In late 2002 this disease
emerged in Hong Kong. Later studies by epidemiologists determined that a
single person, a young man who had traveled from mainland China to Hong
Kong by train, and who had been admitted to Hong Kong's Prince of Wales
Hospital, had infected 50 people, which led to 156 cases in the hospital
alone. Subsequently, the outbreak at the Prince of Wales Hospital led to
a second outbreak in Hong Kong, which ended with the epidemic spreading
to Canada and other countries. Based on these examples, epidemiologists
have become convinced that the severity of epidemics depends
disproportionately on the behavior of a few special individuals,
the [superspreaders](http://www.medterms.com/script/main/art.asp?articlekey=22951) (like the famous [Typhoid Mary](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonelosis#Mar.C3.ADa_Tifoidea)).

![](Mallon-Mary.jpg)

But a more careful analysis of the SARS case reveals that the real cause
of the problem was a misdiagnosis of pneumonia when the patient was
admitted to the hospital. Instead of being kept in isolation (the
standard procedure when receiving a patient with an unknown respiratory
virus), the patient was placed in an open ward with poor air
circulation. What's more, since he had been misdiagnosed with pneumonia,
he was put on a bronchial ventilator, which started spreading a large
quantity of viral particles into the air around him. The conditions in
the ward, packed with people, meant that a large number of patients and
hospital staff got sick too. These events were very important in the
spread of the disease, at least locally.

Seen this way, the patient doesn't seem as important as the details of
his treatment. Before this, nothing we could have known about the
patient would have made us suspect that there was anything special about
that person, because there is nothing special about that patient.

In the events that came after the outbreak at the Prince of Wales
Hospital, there is nothing special about the people who spread the virus
either. In fact, in the second Hong Kong outbreak, in an apartment
building, there is a sequence of other additional elements, such as a
leak in the building's plumbing, that explains the spread of the virus
(in this case a resident of the building had been treated at the
hospital, got infected with SARS there, and was suffering from diarrhea
when he returned to his apartment).

As you can see, [common sense](/en/blog/lnds/2011/06/28/the-paradox-of-common-sense/)
fools us once again, by generating plausible causes in which special
people, or people with special attributes, appear as the ones
responsible for extraordinary events. This, added to the fact that many
other details and pieces of evidence tend to get discarded, ends up
leaving a single thread that explains everything.
