Measure and react: that is basically the strategy of the famous
fashion chain Zara. Rather than trying to predict what people
will be wearing next week, at Zara they simply accept that they have
no idea. The strategy is to observe what people are wearing
in different cities and from that generate a series of ideas that
might work. They prepare a few sample garments, send them to the
stores and watch what sells and what doesn't. What doesn't sell
gets dropped, inventory is kept low, and the successful products
go into mass production and are quickly distributed to the whole
chain (thanks to spectacular
[logistics](http://hbswk.hbs.edu/archive/4652.html)).

If we cannot predict what is coming, a reactive strategy can be
a good alternative for handling uncertainty. Measure and react
is what [Henry Mintzberg](http://www.mintzberg.org/) called
**emergent strategy**.

In the late 1990s Mintzberg was talking about the fall of strategic
planning, and he identified three fallacies in it:

1.  **The fallacy of prediction**: the future environment cannot
    be predicted, since it is impossible to predict the behavior of
    competitors.

2.  **The fallacy of detachment**: strategy formulation
    cannot be separated from the process of management; a planning
    department cannot capture all the information needed for
    strategy formulation. Nor should it be a formal, periodic
    process; it is a dynamic one.

3.  **The fallacy of formalization**: formal strategic
    planning procedures are insufficient to cope with the
    constant changes in the environment. For that, organizations
    need informal systems and must promote learning
    by bringing thought and action together.[^1]

Mintzberg gained a certain notoriety for his harsh criticism of
strategic planning. 
In his book [Managers Not MBAs](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B001UQ6WW8/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=lanaturaledel-20&linkCode=as2&camp=217145&creative=399373&creativeASIN=B001UQ6WW8) he criticizes
the training of managers through a graduate degree like the MBA. He
argues that management cannot be taught at universities, that it
must be learned in daily practice, learning from other managers
or administrators. Mintzberg's position on strategic
management is that we should abandon the idea of setting
long-term plans and learn to react quickly to
what is happening on the ground.

This strategy works very well in the development of
online systems. For example, [Yahoo!](http://www.yahoo.com) used the
concept of user _buckets_ to test the changes it
wanted to roll out on its home page in 2009. When they wanted to test a
new design idea, they randomly selected a small
percentage of the more than 100 million users who use
[Yahoo!](http://www.yahoo.com) as their home page. They measured and observed
the reactions of the users in the bucket and compared them against the
rest of the users; essentially they measured what they clicked on,
how much time they spent on the home page, and so on. If the change was
well received, it went to production on the rest of the site.

Plenty of startups use these lightweight testing techniques.
For example, they create pages with links that lead to a page with
the "excuses for why this particular feature isn't implemented
yet". But they keep track of which links are in highest demand, and
that determines which part of the site gets built first. Sites like
[Google](http://www.google.com), [Microsoft](http://www.microsoft.com) and
[Facebook](http://www.facebook.com) use these techniques to test
new features on their sites and see how they are received.

This is actually pretty old. I remember my father, who 
[was a radio man](https://www.akarru.com/blog/2010/09/23/el-espiritu-de-la-radio/), telling me about the key to the success of the Uruguayan band 
[Los Iracundos](http://www.losiracundos.com.uy/) (famous in Chile for their
song [Puerto Montt](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5TePzHvg_3g)). In
the sixties Los Iracundos racked up an impressive number
of hits and number ones on the pop music
charts. The key was that they wrote a huge number of songs,
which they tested every week on local radio. If the song worked,
then it was recorded and distributed by RCA, their label.

The measure-and-react strategy is behind the mechanisms of
[crowdsourcing](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowdsourcing). There are
news websites that maintain a large pool of contributors
working as volunteers, and that analyze which articles capture the
most interest from their audience, and based on that, together with the analysis of
searches and navigation on the site, decide which topics to write about
in the site's main articles. The same approach is used to moderate
forums and avoid problems like trolling. They keep restricted versions of
the forums, monitored by volunteers who take care
of filtering these small groups before their content is passed on to the
open forums.

All of this is very interesting, and it seems to work, but it forces us to
ask a question: 
[if we rule out predicting the future](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/26/predictions/), 
and we have seen that [we aren't able to satisfactorily explain the
past either](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/12/circular-reasoning/),
can we *"predict the present"*? That is another interesting chapter
of this entertaining exploration.
