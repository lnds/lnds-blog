In the movie Super 8 a young man working at a gas station is listening to music
on his Walkman. He has his back to the street. When the sheriff walks in
he has to tap him to get a reaction. There is a bit of dialogue, where the kid
explains what the Walkman is, and the sheriff makes an annoyed remark
that this is how the trouble starts: put up with kids with speakers in
their ears, and other problems will follow. The old man leaves the store,
the kid puts his headphones back on and goes back to his own thing, paying
no attention to the outside. This is a monster movie, and
of course, while the kid is listening to his Walkman, the poor
sheriff is attacked by the movie's mysterious monster. A lot
happens out there, and only after the power goes out does the kid
realize that something happened.

Super 8 is set in 1979, when these devices were only just
starting to be produced (\*). The sheriff is right, but not in the
sense he means. With the arrival of personal stereos came
an era in which we started isolating ourselves in sound. And when we
shut ourselves inside our personal cloud of sound we miss a lot of
what is around us, important things.

Our ears give us important information, and we have to
take care of them. Silence is wonderful, and necessary; you have to seek it out.
These days people seem to need a personal soundtrack
all the time, and because of that they are losing the ability to listen.

The best proof that we have lost this ability to listen
lies at the root of many of our current conflicts. Today it seems
conflicts escalate way out of proportion, and we often
wonder why they grow instead of shrinking. Because
we dig into rigid positions and refuse to listen to others.
We have simply lost the ability to listen. We want to impose
our own speech, not listen to the other person. We are rude, we don't
keep quiet when the other person is talking, we don't look at them, we are
busy with our own stuff, we don't disconnect from our
computers or cell phones.

I leave you with this reflection by [Julian
Trasure](http://www.juliantreasure.com) on this loss of our
ability to listen. It is a short talk, 7 minutes, and at
the end he proposes 5 exercises we can do to improve our
ability to listen:

Julian Treasure defines listening as making meaning from sound. So
listening is a mental process, a process of extraction. But we
have become impatient. We no longer want oratory, we want sound bites,
catchphrases. We look for fragments; we have grown lazy and don't
want to do the work of extracting the meaning.

The art of conversation is being replaced, dangerously, by
personal broadcasting. We all want to be
radio stations, or TV stations. Listen to me. But if everyone is transmitting,
who is bothering to receive?

How much listening is there in our conversations? The media literally
have to scream at us with their headlines, turning up the volume when
the news comes on, all to win our
attention. It is hard to pay attention to the subtle, to the understated.

It is a serious problem, because listening is our access to understanding.
Conscious listening always creates understanding.

Treasure proposes 5 exercises:

1.  Seek silence. Three minutes of silence a day is a
    wonderful exercise to recalibrate our ears. If you can't
    get silence, then look for some quiet time.

2.  The mixer. In a sound environment, how many channels of sound
    are you hearing? Can you identify them? At a lake, for example,
    how many birds are you hearing? When you listen to music, can you
    tell apart every instrument playing in the piece?

3.  The third exercise is about savoring sound. Even
    mundane sounds can be quite complex. Listen to them
    carefully, appreciate them, notice their subtleties.

4.  This exercise is the most important one: it is about moving your
    listening positions. Learning to use the filters we apply to
    our listening. Moving from active to passive, from reductive to
    expansive, from critical to empathetic.

5.  And finally an acronym: RASA, which is the Sanskrit
    word for **essence, or juice.** RASA is

    -   **R**eceive

    -   **A**ppreciate

    -   **S**ummarize

    -   **A**sk

Let's not lose the ability to listen; it is the key to resolving
our conflicts. If we are going to reform education, let's ask that
listening be taught in our schools.

(\*) Yes, there is an anachronism in the movie, Walkmans
came out later, but it doesn't matter. See, you're not listening!
