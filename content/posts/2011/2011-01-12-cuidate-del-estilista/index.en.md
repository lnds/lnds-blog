"**Don't ask the barber whether he thinks you need a
haircut**," [Chato advises us](http://manzanamecanica.org/2011/01/no_le_preguntes_al_peluquero.html),
a reflection that grew out of some conversation with patent lawyers.
Well, it's true that we can regard many practitioners of the law
as [the cause of most
evils](/blog/lnds/2006/01/27/la-raiz-de-todos-los-males/),
but let's not get carried away either.

Carlos wrote a [nice post](http://manzanamecanica.org/2011/01/no_le_preguntes_al_peluquero.html),
but the ideas he slips in are dangerous.

> *"El profesor no tiene la cabeza en enseñar, porque el doctor no sale
> de su casa para sanar" ("The teacher's mind isn't on teaching, because the doctor doesn't leave
> his house to heal"), sang Jorge González in the 1980s. Governments
> ask the teachers' unions what the best way to improve
> education is. The teachers' unions answer that
> raising their salaries is an excellent measure, but that evaluating
> teachers and demanding excellence from them would be disastrous for the students.
> That professional associations exist is not the problem. The problem
> is that you cannot assume they will look after the common good, **because they only
> look after the interests of their own**.*

_Doctors, and even more so the pharmaceutical industry, have been
accused of the same thing. Many of them, plain and simple, want us to
keep going to the doctor and buying medicine. Medicines are not
always sold by prescription only as a matter of public
health. Sometimes it is because it suits some people that we need to go to the
doctor to get well, that we cannot fend for ourselves.
Scientists and artists have not escaped the same accusation either:
that they don't need an environment free of pressure because their search for
knowledge and beauty requires it, but simply because
anyone would like to have all the time in the world to gaze at their own
navel."_

(the bold is mine)

It is hard to disagree with these arguments, but they are dangerous,
because this is the kind of argument that erodes the power of
unions, undermines the legitimacy of professional bodies,
gets in the way of democratic debate and, in the end, damages the social fabric. They
stay at the accident and never get to the essence of the problems. They are also
caricatures, and reasoning from caricatures leads to
naive, almost childish solutions.

But the other thing that bothers me about Carlos's article is that mix of
arrogance and naivety:

> "For this, for all of this, what the people who promote free
> software have done is so important. This is a group of people with
> a very lucrative profession who have decided to reveal a great secret
> of their trade. That secret is that even when there is little or no
> money involved, there will still be software. Because programming is
> a compulsion, because it is extremely satisfying to do, and
> because there is an extraordinary and wonderful imbalance between the time
> it takes to write a program and the impact you can have if
> millions of people use it."

The images that come to mind when I read this sort of thing range
from nuns rescuing prostitutes in Amsterdam's Red Light District to
young environmentalists, settled in a remote village in Sub-Saharan
Africa, teaching children how to use energy-saving light
bulbs.

Truth is, I don't know whether I should tell some young programmers
I know that, since they practice such a lucrative profession, the
right thing would be to donate their time to an open source project, which
will then be used by the very company that employs them so it doesn't have to pay for
licenses or maintenance contracts. I can't bring myself to go that far.

Besides, tell me there is no arrogance in declaring how good we
programmers are because we have shown the world the way(\*):

> *Once this secret is revealed, what is left for the rest of
> society is not to ignore what it has heard, but to take advantage of it. And
> so you can see there is no trick behind it, free software is not only
> free, its code is open and **it is provided under a legal agreement that
> gives every possible guarantee**. Obviously there are people in my
> profession who would prefer things to be otherwise, but
> there is enough critical mass for there to be as many voluntary
> waivers of our guild's monopoly rights as are
> needed. **Maybe someday other professions will do the same**.*

(again, the bold is mine)

(It seems Chato hasn't read the part that says "This program is
distributed in the hope that it will be useful, but WITHOUT ANY
WARRANTY" (GPL v3), it seems ;)

Enough. Let's not be naive, because in the end, who
benefits from these free culture ideas? The *Cloud
Computing Lords, _as Jaron Lanier would say_, like Google, like **Yahoo**.

What greater benefit for a company that lives off information than
for all information to be free, open, accessible? Because that is better for
the business model (which boils everything down to selling advertising (\*\*)). And
who demands anything in return from these companies? Where is the source
code of their essential algorithms, of the core of their business? No, that
is hidden away safely in the cloud. And to muddy the waters,
they release what has already become a commodity. After all, that's what
we have geniuses for, inventing for us, happily working 20 hours
a day, living on candy and soda.

Ideas have value when they are secret. The people who pay those
lucrative salaries to these brilliant but naive minds know that very well,
while those minds spend their time feeding these pipe dreams of
freedom. But besides, the [source code doesn't matter](/en/blog/lnds/2010/09/08/its-not-the-code-stupid/),
because it no longer has any value, so it makes no difference whether it is handed over, given away,
passed around, do whatever you want with it.

I will keep asking my barber whether I need a
haircut, because I like him and he needs the work. The ones I don't like are
those stylists who work at those international fashion
style chains and come to tell me what we need.
