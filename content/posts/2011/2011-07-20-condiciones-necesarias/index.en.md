> Security in human systems we're told will always, always last \--
> Sting, History Teach us Nothing

In all the previous articles the central idea was to show that much of
what we call [common sense](/en/blog/lnds/2011/06/28/the-paradox-of-common-sense/)
is some form of [circular reasoning](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/12/circular-reasoning/),
that many common-sense explanations [are wrong](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/10/intuition/), and that this is
shown when they are confronted with experiments or facts derived from
the real study of the behavior of human groups.

The Mona Lisa is the most famous painting in the world because it has
the attributes of the Mona Lisa. A few people are capable of promoting a
trend, or setting a fashion. Many common-sense explanations may well be
true, but what they really do is tell us that what happened happened,
and not something else. Since these explanations can only be built once
the outcome is known, we are never sure whether they are really
explanations or simple descriptions.

The problem is that, unlike other sciences, where we can test hypotheses
in controlled experiments that we can repeat over and over, with
historical events that is not possible. The experiment happens only
once. History happens only once. The outcome of a historical event has a
lot to do with the
[micro interactions between people](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/13/influences/), and as
[we have seen](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/12/circular-reasoning/) it
is not possible to know these in detail, or their distribution, and the
explanation stays hidden from the eyes of the observers.

## Sampling bias

One of the reasons we can't find satisfactory explanations for
historical events is that we are bad at selecting the relevant
information. The problem lies in the things we pay attention to. We tend
to ignore the events that don't happen, or we ignore things that happen
very frequently. We single out the times we run into a friend at the
airport, but not the times that doesn't happen. The times we miss the
bus, we remember or notice more than the many times the bus pulls up
moments after we arrive. We notice a soccer player's scoring streaks,
but not the great majority of players who have an ordinary season. In
short, we notice the interesting facts. Obviously; it's only natural.
But the problem is that we project that natural tendency onto our
explanations of phenomena, ignoring the rest of the information.

If we want to explain how people get rich, or the key to some companies'
success, we look at the millionaires, or the successful companies, to
identify the attributes they share. That is what financial magazines and
newspapers do when they prepare their profiles of star executives or
prosperous businessmen. But it turns out that if we looked at the
thousands of other cases of people who aren't rich, or who haven't had
"success", we would discover that they exhibit many of the same
attributes.

> "The only way to identify the attributes that set the successful
> apart from the unsuccessful is to consider both kinds, and look for
> the differences systematically. But since we are interested in
> success, it seems pointless, or uninteresting, to worry about the lack
> of success. So we infer that certain attributes are related to success
> when in fact they are equally related to failure." \-- Duncan Watts[^1]

This is [sampling bias](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesgo_muestral).
To understand the problems this kind of distortion causes, let's look at
an example. In a major plane crash that occurred during landing, the
investigating commission determined five factors that contributed to the
accident: first, the plane's radio was faulty, and failed at a critical
stage of the approach; second, the pilot and the navigator were
fatigued, having had few hours of sleep in the days before the accident;
third, the fog was especially thick that night, and visibility was poor;
fourth, some instructions from the tower were contradictory, which
caused confusion, and it wasn't clear which runway was to be used;
fifth, as a result of all of the above, and of the confusing
instructions, the ground operator got confused and, given the stressful
situation, understood that a different runway was to be used and forgot
to clear a vehicle that was in the middle of the landing area. The
investigation concluded that the combination of these five elements was
the cause of the accident.

Later a psychologist analyzed the case and pointed out that although the
explanation is reasonable, familiar and consistent with plane crashes in
general, these same factors show up consistently in many similar
situations and no accidents occur. Graphically this can be represented
like this:

{{<figure caption="Figure 2: The same causes, and a plane crash doesn't always occur" src="Sesgo1.png">}}

In other words, these factors are necessary conditions, but not
sufficient ones, for producing an accident. Without them an accident is
extremely unlikely, but their being present does not mean an accident
will happen. Yet when the accident does occur, all the other arrows
disappear, and the world collapses into a single explanation: the
combination of these five factors causes the accident. That is not
really an explanation; we are only reporting what happened.

If in figure 2 the combination of factors looks like a bad predictor of
the outcome, when the accident occurs we move to the scenario of figure
1, in which the same factors become the perfect explanation of the
accident. The investigation helps find the necessary conditions for the
accident, which is a good thing, because it lets us take preventive
measures to avoid accidents. But the temptation to use these conditions
as sufficient conditions for an accident leaves our intuition in very
poor shape for explaining accidents.

And the same thing happens with all kinds of rare events, as when
[an armed person walks into the metro and kills its passengers](https://www.biobiochile.cl/2011/07/17/al-menos-4-heridos-en-tiroteo-en-estacion-de-metro-plaza-de-maipu-en-santiago.shtml).
We can find all the attributes that explain this person's behavior:
psychological profile, past, history, psychiatric problems, or alcohol,
depression, etc. A pile of necessary conditions for this kind of attack
to happen. But there are thousands of people with the same problems, and
they don't go around shooting people in the metro. We simply cannot
explain the reasons that lead a person to act this way.
