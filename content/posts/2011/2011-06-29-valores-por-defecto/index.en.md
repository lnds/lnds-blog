The chart below shows the consent rate for organ donation in 11 European
countries:

![](/images/2011/06/DonacionDeOrganos.png)

What stands out is that we have practically two sharply defined groups,
two clear extremes: the first 4 countries with very low rates, and the
other extreme where practically the entire population has consented to
donate their organs when they die.

But what explains the difference between one extreme and the other?

Consider countries 4 and 5. Here they are in more detail:

![](/images/2011/06/DosPaises.png)

Let's try to come up with some **common-sense theory** that explains why
one country has a consent rate of 12% and the other 99.98%.

That is the question Duncan Watts put to his students at Columbia
University: formulate a theory that explains this difference between
country 4 and country 5 in our chart.

Some of the opinions the students offered were:

> -   Maybe one country is more secular, and the other is highly
>     religious.
>
> -   Perhaps one country has more advanced medical care and the success
>     rate of organ transplants is higher in one country than in the
>     other.
>
> -   Maybe the accident rate is higher in one of the countries, which
>     means more organs are available for transplants.
>
> -   Or perhaps one country has a highly socialist culture, emphasizing
>     the importance of the community over that of individuals, while in
>     the other individual rights come first

The truth is that the explanations are pretty good, until we're told
that country 4 is Germany and country 5 is Austria.

What could make Germany and Austria so different?

-   Is there some difference in their educational and legal systems that
    we don't know about?

-   Maybe there was a media campaign, or a big event that shook Austrian
    society?

-   Does it have something to do with World War II?

None of Watts's students could venture a convincing explanation, but
what was clear is that whatever it was, it had to be something really
big. And surely many of you don't know the real cause of this
phenomenon, and the explanation is absurdly simple:

> *In Austria, the default option is to be an organ donor, while in
> Germany the default option is \*\*not \*\*to be an organ donor.*

This explains the enormous difference among all these countries. The
details are in the study "Do defaults save lives?" by Eric Johnson and
Daniel Goldstein (available at this address: <https://webs.wofford.edu/pechwj/Do%20Defaults%20Save%20Lives.pdf>)

"In the case of organ donation, European countries have one of two
default policies. In presumed-consent states, people are organ donors
unless they register not to be, and in explicit-consent countries,
nobody is an organ donor without first registering to be one". And guess
what happens. Every country with an opt-out mechanism for organ donation
has a higher donation rate. (\*)

Understanding the influence of default options matters, because
understanding how we make choices affects virtually all of our
explanations of social, economic and political behavior.

One of the problems with many economic models is that, in order to
understand market behavior, economists assume that people make rational
decisions. In general, theories of rational choice assume that people
prefer what is best for them. This view is very attractive, because it
implies that all human behavior can be understood in terms of
individuals trying to satisfy their preferences: "I vote because I care
about taking part in politics, and when I vote I choose the candidate
who will best serve my interests." Although we know that's not how it
is, at least not in Chile ;) But it's still possible to find a reason
why there are people who keep voting.

There are many phenomena that seem irrational, or that escape the
rational-choice explanation, in the traditional sense of what we
understand by *rational*. For example, an Israeli school decided that
parents who showed up late to pick up their children would be fined.
What happened is that once the measure was applied, the number of
parents arriving late to pick up their kids went up. Why? Because the
fine relieved the parents' feeling of guilt toward the school staff,
since in essence they figured they were paying for the right to arrive
later. In a certain sense there is a rational choice here, but it isn't
rational in the sense the school staff had in mind, or rational in the
sense a traditional economist would use.

When we study why people make the decisions they make, we have to
analyze the incentives they face when deciding. That way we can find a
"rational" explanation of why they behave the way they do. The problem
is that what we've done is precisely to "rationalize" the behavior, and
rationalizing something is not the same as understanding it, or
explaining it.

> *"Rationalizing human behavior is an exercise in simulation. In our
> minds we simulate what it would be like to be the person whose
> behavior we want to understand. Only when we can imagine this
> simulated version of ourselves responding in the manner of the
> individual in question do we really feel that we have understood the
> behavior under study." \[1\]*

And yet, as in the organ donor example, our mental simulations tend to
ignore certain kinds of factors that turn out to be very important.
Defaults are part of the environment in which the decision maker
operates, and they affect behavior in a way that is invisible to the
conscious mind, and so they are absent from common-sense explanations of
behavior. And defaults are only the tip of the iceberg, as we'll see in
the next articles of this series.

### Notes

(\*) We are talking about consent rates for being a donor, not rates of
actual donors. In Chile, for example, the opt-out model applies (since
2010), that is, people are organ donors unless they explicitly refuse to
have their organs donated. Whether the donation actually happens is
another matter: the main cause of non-donation of organs in Chile is
refusal by the family
(see <https://www.trasplante.cl/noticias/2011/img/memoria_corporacion.pdf>)


\[1\] Taken from the book [Everything Is Obvious: \*Once You Know the
Answer](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0385531680/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=lanaturaledel-20&linkCode=as2&camp=217145&creative=399373&creativeASIN=0385531680) by Duncan Watts.
