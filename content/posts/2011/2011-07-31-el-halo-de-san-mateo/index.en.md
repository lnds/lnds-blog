For several years Fortune magazine saw Enron as "America's most
innovative company", right up to its spectacular
bankruptcy in 2001. A very similar case is La Polar, the retail chain, in Chile. In
April 2010 Pablo Alcalde presented "La Polar's successful strategy"
at the eClass seminar run by La Tercera / UAI (here is the
[video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iF08GL9zSHI)).

It is very interesting to listen to some of Alcalde's words,
especially in light of what we now know, and also from the perspective of everything
we have covered in this series of articles:

> "[\...\] the amount of analysis we have done on our
> consumers. We have gone to their homes, we have seen how they live, we have
> done sociological studies, we know what they think, we know what
> they do on Sundays, what they do with their time, why they go to
> shopping malls, why they don't go to others, what's going on with Transantiago, what's
> going on with their kids, what their aspirations are, how they have evolved,
> etc., etc.

> [...] I define the mix based on consumer
behavior, and according to that lineup I say I need five thousand square
meters of sales floor, or six thousand square meters, and I know I am
optimizing sales per square meter and per salesperson, which gives me
a motivated salesperson.

> [...] The customer feels welcome, because he never walks past a
store where he can't buy. That person, that family unit, sees
La Polar as more than a department store, sees it as a life choice..

> [...] What you have, in short, is **trust**, which is the hardest
thing to achieve in life. When you get people to
trust you, you have broken the sound barrier. I believe in La Polar, and since I believe in
La Polar I'm going to buy, I'm going to pay, I take out my insurance, etc. ***And only
then does a more spiritual bond with the customer emerge***".[^1]

The trade press and the business schools presented La
Polar as a brilliant example of a successful strategy, an example of
innovation and leadership. The executives of Enron and La Polar were
held up as role models for other executives. Of course, La
Polar, just like Enron, projected an image of success built on a
fraud. When the trade press, universities or think
tanks evaluate these cases, they fall for what psychologists call the
[Halo Effect](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efecto_halo).

## **The Halo Effect**

The Halo Effect is a cognitive bias in which we extend our
perception of one particular trait to other traits that are not
necessarily related. For example, if a person is
physically attractive we tend to think they are also
intelligent. [John
Adams](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Adams) once remarked that [George
Washington](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington) was
considered the natural choice for leader simply because he always
turned out to be the tallest person in the room.

![](John_Ausonius.jpg)

Our judgment of how [John Ausonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ausonius) looks
will depend on whether we think he is a) a movie star, b) a prize-winning
scientist or c) a serial killer.

The problem is that companies, or people, can project
these images of high performance, create an aura (the halo) that skews the
analysis made by third parties. We get carried away by the image, to
put it simply.

In one experiment several groups of participants were asked to
do the financial analysis of a fictitious firm. Afterwards each
group was scored on its performance and asked to comment on how
well it had worked as a team on various metrics, such as
group cohesion, communication and motivation. The groups that received
a higher score considered themselves to be indeed more
cohesive, motivated and communicative, unlike the groups that
received lower scores, which came up with the harshest
self-criticism. The catch is that each group's score was assigned at random
by the experimenter; there was no difference in performance between the groups
with the highest scores and those with the lowest. The appearance of
superior results leads to the illusion of high functioning. Mind you, there are
no outside observers here explaining the performance; it is the groups
themselves evaluating themselves according to the result they supposedly got. If
they tell you you're doing well, you'll believe you're doing well; if they tell
you you did badly, you'll find every flaw in yourself.

The halo effect distorts your judgment of your own performance inside
your own head. The evaluation is not determined by the quality of the process;
rather, it is the way we evaluate the outcome that determines
how the process gets evaluated.

There is nothing wrong with evaluating a process by its results, as long as
those results are repeatable and we can run several evaluations,
as a way to mitigate the halo effect. But often there are
processes we can only try once, and in that case what we must
do is focus our effort on evaluating and improving what we do
while we are doing it. There are techniques for this, which
we will present later on.

## Talent and luck 

The halo effect can also trap us in the negative direction. Consider the
scandal caused by the huge compensation paid to financial
traders, especially after a crisis. The outrage is not
that executives get paid a lot of money, because we know they
get paid a lot of money, but that they get paid so much money for such
disastrous results.

Take the example of those executives who in 2009 qualified
for their bonuses. Did they deserve those bonuses in the middle of the
crisis? After all, they had not caused the crisis, so why
should they be penalized for other people's mistakes? In the words of an
AIG executive at the time: "I earned that money, and I have nothing
to do with the bad things that happened to AIG"[^2]. 
The argument executives make is that they are necessary, because
in the end they are the ones who generate profitable business, and if they were not paid
these bonuses they could move to the competition and take the
big deals with them. But this could be the halo effect
all over again.

Imagine this experiment. Every year we flip a coin. If it comes up heads we
will call it a good year and you get a bonus of 10 million; if it comes up
tails it is a bad year and you get nothing. The interesting thing about this game is
that as an executive your expected value is to make 5 million a
year, while for the employer the gains are offset by the
losses: your contribution as an executive will be zero.

Surely the AIG executive, and several others, will say that their performance
is not a matter of luck, that it is the product of hard work, of long
hours, of their skills and experience. But their colleagues
said the same thing before the 2008 meltdown. So why should we
believe him any more than his colleagues?

Telling talent from luck is a big problem. And there are a lot of
people who get results by pure luck. To be sure, and
to avoid falling for the halo effect, we need a measure that goes directly to
the person's skills, rather than to results that may be
determined by forces beyond individual control.

## The Matthew Effect 

In sports we get the chance to measure athletes' talent
repeatedly. Not only do we offset the halo effect by having
several possible measurements, we also try to make sure that every time they step onto
the field the conditions are even for all opponents, by
enforcing the rules of the game. It would bother us if
Djokovic walked into a match with an extra serve
just for being the top player on the ATP tour, or if the team
leading the league were given a two-goal head start in the final
stages of the championship.

But life is characterized by what sociologists call the
**Matthew Effect**, which comes from the parable of the talents,
specifically from the Gospel according to Saint Matthew: "to him who has more, more will be
given, and from him who has less, it will be taken away and given to the one who has
more."[^3] In the biblical context the
[talents](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talento_(moneda)) are coins,
they are money, so in a literal sense that verse is about wealth,
hence the phrase "the rich get richer and the poor get poorer". The
word [talent](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talento_(aptitud))
acquired its current meaning from this parable (the idea that
we should do our best with the talents we have been given), so
the verse takes on another meaning as well, one that matches the idea of the
sociologist who coined the term, [Robert
Merton](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_K._Merton), since he
was referring to success in general.

{{<figure caption="Robert K. Merton, the sociologist who coined the concept of the Matthew Effect" src="Robert_K_Merton.jpg">}}

Early success in an individual's career confers a certain
structural advantage that makes subsequent successes more
likely, regardless of intrinsic ability. In science, for
example, researchers who land at top-tier universities
have more opportunities to publish papers and get funding
than those at second- or third-tier universities. The
result is that two individuals who might be roughly
comparable at the start of their careers can experience dramatically
different levels of success after 5 or 10 years, with no
difference other than having been hired by different institutions. And from
there on things get more unequal. Because
successful scientists tend to receive a disproportionate share of the
credit for anything they are associated with, as
when they write papers with unknown students, who may
have done most of the work, or even had the central
idea. In other words, when a scientist becomes a star he not only
gets more funding, he also attracts more collaborators, which can lead
to his getting credit beyond what he actually deserves[^4].
Robert Merton's [original article](https://www.garfield.library.upenn.edu/merton/matthew1.pdf) 
talks about precisely this phenomenon in science.

But sociologists have established that the Matthew Effect shows up in other
careers too. Early success brings you prominence and
recognition, which gives you more opportunities to succeed, more
resources to succeed with, and the likelihood that later successes
will be attributed to you without your having had much to do with them.
And isolating the cumulative effect of those successes from innate
ability, or hard work, is very difficult.

If we take people with similar characteristics, their
fortunes will diverge, according to Merton's theory. It has been observed
that college students who graduated in years of
economic depression earn less on average than those who
graduated in boom years. And not just during those years: the
difference persists for decades.

We certainly don't like these effects, because we aspire to a
meritocratic society, one where the most talented people, or those who
work the hardest, are the ones who succeed. It may be true that the incompetent
rarely succeed, or that especially
talented people are unlikely to suffer great failures. But few of us fall
at those extremes. For most people, chance combined with accumulated
advantage can mean that ordinary people end up
doing quite well, or badly. Since every individual story is unique, we can always
convince ourselves that the outcome is somehow the
product of that person's unique characteristics. The important
conclusion is that when we evaluate a person's success based on
the measures society imposes (wealth, prizes, honorary
titles) instead of their abilities, or their skill at getting things done,
we are fooling ourselves. Always measure a person's abilities
by their actual performance under conditions that depend only on their
own abilities, not by results that don't depend on them, or
on the random circumstances that put them in a position
to accumulate advantages. Otherwise you will always be
fooled by the combined effect of the Halo of Saint Matthew :wink:
