> "The best way to predict the future is to invent it" -- [Alan
> Kay](/en/blog/lnds/2010/05/26/alan-kay/)

The story of [Betamax](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betamax) versus
[VHS](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS) is well known. Sony focused
on picture quality, and developed a closed format. Matsushita,
on the other hand, developed a lower-quality technology with
VHS, but one that allowed longer recordings, long enough to record
entire movies. On top of that the standard was open, which let
multiple manufacturers compete at building devices,
and that drove prices down. When the video rental market
exploded, VHS held a slight lead, and through a
process of cumulative advantage it ended up winning.

{{<figure caption="Betamax player" src="Betamax.jpg">}}

But Sony had not conceived of the video recorder as a device for
watching rented movies. In the Japanese company's vision, people
would use the Betamax to store television shows. In fact,
back then there had been experiments with movie rentals
that had failed. Sony's vision was not crazy. Even so,
once it was clear that people were using VHS to watch movies,
it developed the Beta II format, with longer recording time, but it was already
too late. The flaw in the strategy was that shift in demand,
which was faster and more unexpected than anyone in the industry
could have anticipated.

Years later Sony made another big technology bet with its
MiniDisc players. Since they did not want to make the same
mistake twice, they tried to avoid the failures of Betamax. MiniDiscs had the
capacity to record entire albums, and with the creation of Sony
Music they made sure to acquire a content catalog that would guarantee
the success of the new medium. MiniDiscs had several technical
advantages over the CDs of the time: MiniDiscs were recordable,
CD burners were more expensive back then, and
MiniDiscs were smaller and tougher. It was a medium
destined to be a bestseller. So what happened? In essence, the Internet.
Memory costs dropped, and people could store the music
they wanted on their personal computers. High-speed internet
connections started to spread, peer-to-peer file sharing
systems appeared, then pen drives,
flash drives, websites for downloading to portable devices,
formats like mp3. Nobody could foresee what would happen with the internet.
Sony did the best it could to avoid the failures of the past, it learned
from its failed strategy, it tried to anticipate the future, it even tried to
invent the future ([Alan Kay](/en/blog/lnds/2010/05/26/alan-kay/) style), but it was
defeated by forces it could neither predict nor control.

The surprising part is that Apple got it right, with its iPods and the
iTunes store, even though it used the same kind of strategy as
Betamax, similar even to its own past experience in the 1980s when
it competed against the PC. The iPod was a bigger, more expensive device, with
a closed architecture that Apple refused to license, running
proprietary software, and on top of that it was resisted by most
content providers. So why did Apple do better than Sony?

{{<figure caption="First-generation iPod" src="ipod.jpg">}}

Apple had a great product, just like Sony. Both companies looked
into the future as best they could, to spot the technology
trends. They made their decisions, set their strategies, and
executed their plans brilliantly. The only difference is that
Apple's strategy worked and Sony's did not. This is the strategy
paradox, according to Michael Raynor, described in his book 
[The Strategy Paradox](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B000OI0G5C/ref=as_li_qf_sp_asin_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=lanaturaledel-20&linkCode=as2&camp=217145&creative=399373&creativeASIN=B000OI0G5C).
The success of a strategy depends on whether the vision of the future laid out
at the start turns out to be right or not, something that is not only impossible to know,
but impossible.

The solution to the strategy paradox, according to Raynor, lies in
openly acknowledging that there are limits to what can be predicted, and
therefore developing methods for planning within those limits. In
particular, he recommends incorporating "strategic uncertainties", that
is, laying out possible scenarios, speculations, hypothetical
stories about the future. Raynor's proposal is quite radical: in
his view the heads of companies should be
permanently thinking through future scenarios, and should drop
operational management, leaving it to a lower layer that
takes care of optimizing and operating the established strategies.

Managing strategic uncertainties can be an interesting
exercise, one that lets us handle various possible scenarios, and
perhaps get ahead of some of the future's surprises. But all of this
involves making predictions, and we have already seen that making predictions is
problematic. If planning is a problem, there must be another way, and there
is one, a way that puts less emphasis on anticipating the future, or the multiple
possible futures, and concentrates more on reacting to the present. That is
something we will look at soon.
