My father, like any good electronics man, had a lot of interesting
tools, some of them dangerous, as my brother and I discovered very
painfully. Other tools were intriguing, strange, and complicated.
Pliers, screwdrivers, voltmeters or testers, oscilloscopes or plain
soldering irons, boards for mounting circuits, a pair of magnifying
glasses with a visor and a loupe that were good for inspecting circuits,
or for dressing up as an alien. But the best one, my favorite, was that
"paralyzing dart launcher used in the clone wars of the 25th century in
the hills of Chuquicamata": the piston desoldering pump. Here is a photo
of the closest thing I could find to that wonderful futuristic device,
futuristic at least for an 11-year-old boy in 1977 (now you'll
understand [why I liked Super 8 so much](https://www.akarru.com/blog/2011/08/07/super-8/) )

{{<figure caption="a 25th-century paralyzing dart launcher" src="dessoldador.jpg">}}

Like every good technician, my father took care of his tools, and I
certainly don't think he was amused when my brother and I "kidnapped"
them for our space adventures. There were tools he got from his job, and
others he bought himself. Someone who works with tools takes acquiring
them very seriously, and whenever he could he invested in quality
tools.

There are cheap tools and very expensive ones. We have all bought tools,
and we know we can buy a screwdriver that won't last beyond some quick
repair. But if you're serious about this business of using tools, then
you'll invest a bit more time and money. After all, a good tool will not
only last, it will also save you time in your work.

The other important thing is the space where you do your work, where you
use those tools. My father had his own space, a place for himself, his
sacred place, where he was reunited with his tools, just as we humans
have done since the days when we were hunter-gatherers.

As Joseph Campbell told Bill Moyers in [The Power of
Myth](http://amzn.to/YiV0UG):

> "This is ... an absolute necessity for anybody today. You must have a
> room, or a certain hour a day or so, where you do not know what was in
> the newspapers that morning, you don't know who your friends are, you
> don't know what you owe to anybody, you don't know what anybody owes
> to you, but a place where you can simply experience and bring forth
> what you are and what you might be."
>
> "This is the place of creative incubation. And first you may find that
> nothing's happening there, but if you have a sacred place and use it
> and take advantage of it, something will happen."

{{<figure caption="Joseph Campbell" src="JosephCampbell.png">}}

For me that sacred place is at home, where I'm writing this right now,
with my mug and a little mint tea to keep me company, and nobody
interrupting me. It's me, my thoughts, a keyboard and 1920x1080 pixels.

This "locus of creation" is as essential as our tools. It is the place
where our spears, stone axes, plows, oscilloscopes, keyboards and
25th-century paralyzing dart launchers are kept, like sacred objects
that go with us on our adventures.

From the spear points of the [Clovis
culture](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultura_clovis) to our modern
particle accelerators, we have sought to perfect our tools. We are Homo
sapiens and Homo faber at the same time, or at least those of us who
alter the world to adapt it to our needs and desires are.

{{<figure caption="Clovis point" src="LanzaClovis.jpg">}}

I imagine the fascination felt by those first hominids who carved those
ancient stones in the rivers of Zambia, the oldest samples of our
technological past. We know that the great apes are able to use tools,
and recently we have discovered that some of them make them (although it
seems that's because they learned from us). Still, it seems we are the
only beings who have turned this ability to build our tools into an
evolutionary advantage that has allowed us to dominate (for better or
worse) this planet.

We have the power to create and to destroy stored in our tools. That's
how important they are to us. I don't think there is a human being who
doesn't use a tool of some kind. We can say this really is one of the
essential aspects of what makes us human. Even language could be
considered a tool, probably the most important one we have created.

## **Mastery**

Mastery of an art or craft is only reached when you design and manage to
build your own tools. Until you have reached that stage you are only an
apprentice in your profession.

We programmers have the advantage that we can build powerful tools using
something like the sacred space I described earlier. With keyboards and
pixels we can create universes of infinite complexity, [as Weizenbaum tells us](/en/blog/lnds/2011/10/11/creators-of-universes/),
tools that let us perform miracles, or automate routine tasks, that let
us create things that didn't exist.

Recently a friend won an important prize using the tools he has
created. I'm talking about [Álvaro Graves](http://graves.cl/about.html),
who won the [Health Metadata Challenge](http://www.health2news.com/2013/02/20/winners-of-health-data-platform-challenges/),
a contest that seeks to develop new technological capabilities using the
open data repositories of the United States Department of Health.

{{<figure src="AlvaroGraves.jpg" caption="Alvaro Graves">}}

This is remarkable, and it shows the power of our profession, which I
consider, without any embarrassment, one of the most important that
human beings have created, and probably one of those that will
profoundly change our society (as it is already doing).

So there's your challenge. It's not enough to acquire your tools, own
them, and master them. You will only begin to reach mastery of your
craft when you are able to build your own tools, and even create the
tools that others are going to use.
