I like [Netflix](http://www.netflix.com). I don't like TV, meaning
today's broadcast television. I stopped watching it a long time ago, and
I used to be a real TV junkie, but its reality shows and its awful
newscasts wore me out years back. I grew up in the seventies, when
television meant, at least in Chile, Yankee series like Star Trek, The
Six Million Dollar Man, Bonanza, and "Tardes de Cine", the afternoon
movie slot, with Abbott and Costello or Dean Martin and Jerry Lewis
pictures, and every now and then some horror classic, like The Mummy
with Boris Karloff, or Dracula with Bela Lugosi. I even remember
watching Hitchcock's Psycho on Televisión Nacional one summer night when
I was about thirteen or fourteen. So the movies I saw weren't that bad
:wink: At least back when television was public, for real.

![](house-of-cards.jpg)

That's why I like Netflix, because I find old series there, but I've
also given the service a chance, and little by little its catalog has
started to interest me more. I've found a few gems, not-so-old movies
that probably never made it to our theaters, or I get to catch up on
series I couldn't watch when they were on cable, like Galactica or
Breaking Bad.

But there are two more reasons why I like Netflix. One is that they have
entered the original production phase, a move Amazon and YouTube are
making too. And the other reason is that they have a remarkable team of
developers.

This post is about the first reason. The technology side, and how they
manage their technology team, will be left for the next post.

In February Netflix premiered the series [House of
Cards](http://www.imdb.es/title/tt1856010/), a political thriller set in
Washington DC. It is produced by the talented film director David
Fincher (Seven, Fight Club, Zodiac, The Social Network), who also
directs the first episodes, and by Kevin Spacey, who plays the lead. A
sort of Richard III (you have to read Shakespeare, kids), scheming,
ambitious, and making the viewer his accomplice by speaking straight to
the camera, just as he does in the promotional video for the series,
which is a scene from the first episode:

{{<youtube ULwUzF1q5w4 >}}

It's a very well made series, but the remarkable thing is the way it has
been released. Unlike other, more "traditional" networks such as HBO
(Game of Thrones) or AMC (Mad Men), in this case Netflix released the
entire first season, all 13 episodes, on February 1,
2013. I like this series, and I recommend it, and if anyone wants to
discuss the plot with me, the comments are down below ;-)
(just be kind to everyone else and give a warning when you're about to
reveal part of the plot for those who haven't seen it (spoiler alerts)).

Like many of us who no longer watch television passively, what I look
for is complete series available over the internet. I don't like
depending on the cable schedule, I want to watch them when I have time,
at my own pace, and I usually like watching them in "marathons". That's
why "pirate" sites like Cuevana or Series Yonkis are so popular,
especially in Latin America. And the people at Netflix understand this
very well, as you can tell from the words of Reed Hastings, the
company's CEO, in [an interview in EMOL from
last year:](http://www.emol.com/noticias/tecnologia/2012/08/29/557934/reed-hastings-sonamos-con-tener-10-millones-de-usuarios-de-netflix-en-latinoamerica.html)

> One of the problems in the Latin American market is piracy, which
> offers new content for free, albeit illegally. How do you convince
> people that Netflix is a good option?
>
> "It's challenging, with Cuevana and other sites that look very
> professional. But Cuevana at night is very difficult, and they don't
> have the money to invest and improve the system. It's like bottled
> water. Not everyone is going to pay for it, not everyone can pay for
> it, but it's healthier. What we have to do is make streaming work
> perfectly, while expanding the selection. Some people will stay with
> piracy, others will convert. The one good thing about piracy is that
> it introduces people to the concept of video over the internet."
>
> Do content producers see Netflix as a way to reach Latin America
> legally?
>
> "Yes. They are the ones who lose the most to piracy, so they have
> supported us. But they want us to pay. They like what we do, but they
> want us to pay."

It's true that there is a problem with the film industry's attitude.
They don't realize that by being so closed-minded they are losing a lot
of money. That's why in Latin America we still can't get certain content
that is available in the US or Canada, and meanwhile, people keep
visiting "pirate" streaming sites.

With more than 30 million subscribers, Netflix now feels confident
enough to create original material, and in doing so it sidesteps many of
the restrictions imposed by the greedy studios.

I, for one, happily pay the 8 dollars for the service. It seems like a
reasonable price, I get content that satisfies me, and I watch it when I
want. Yes, it's true that they analyze my behavior and my content
consumption habits, but that doesn't worry me much if in the end they
give me content that fits my tastes. So far, at least, Netflix has
proven more effective than the old People Meter at giving me quality
programming, something that no longer exists on television. This comes
from a top-notch software development team, which is the side of Netflix
I like the most, but I'll talk about that in the next post.
