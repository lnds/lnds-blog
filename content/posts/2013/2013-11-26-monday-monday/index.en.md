I didn't feel like writing about
[CyberMonday](http://www.cybermonday.cl), but it seems to have become
the thing to do. Several people have already written about it, and although some of the opinions are
interesting, a number of others are very lightweight, despite coming from
sources that ought to dig deeper. So I am going to write
something about this "homegrown festival of electronic consumerism," a
true tradition :).

> Oh Monday morning, Monday morning couldn't guarantee That Monday
> evening you would still be here with me

I wonder: if Cybermonday is so bad, why does it keep drawing
a crowd? And why do people buy anyway, wait anxiously for
midnight to arrive, pull out their credit cards, and start
complaining? Why, if it is such a disaster, are there people who worry so much
about this event and ask whether our country is ready to hold
an event of this kind? National computing pride? The idea that
if they had asked me, I would have done it better?

> Monday Monday, can't trust that day, Monday Monday, sometimes it just
> turns out that way

Everybody hates Monday, even Mama Cass, so why are we
surprised that we hate Monday, and Cyber Monday even more?

But let's get to the point. This Monday and Tuesday, for the third time, the
Santiago Chamber of Commerce organized an event called CyberMonday,
an imitation of the initiative of the same name in the United States.

Around this time of year, up north, there is a retail event with an
ominous name: Black Friday. The fourth Thursday of November is a holiday
in the United States, Thanksgiving Day, and department stores take advantage
of the Friday and Saturday that follow to
offer end-of-season sales. The term originated in the
1960s and was apparently coined by the Philadelphia police, who
hated that weekend because of the congestion caused by the
shoppers flocking to the downtown stores.

The first CyberMonday in the United States was held in 2005. By then
the behavior of online shoppers had been studied,
and it turned out that on the Monday after Thanksgiving Day purchases went up
on the e-commerce websites of the time. The term first appeared
on the site Shop.org.

According to comScore, in 2012 this event
[meant sales of 1.46 billion dollars](http://www.comscore.com/Insights/Press_Releases/2012/12/Cyber_Monday_Headlines_Cyber_Week_as_Three_Individual_Days_Eclipse_1_Billion_in_Online_Spending).
More than the sales of Black Friday itself, which came to
1.042 billion.

> Oh Monday morning, you gave me no warning of what was to be Oh Monday
> Monday, how yould could leave and not take me.

In 2011 the Santiago Chamber of Commerce organized the first edition
of Cybermonday for Chile. The first year there were several stumbles, since
the sites were not prepared for the event. Still, it was a success
and they decided to do it again. According to SERNAC, the national consumer protection agency,
[in 2012 they received only 73 complaints tied to this event](http://www.lanacion.cl/disminuyeron-reclamos-por-cyber-monday-chile-solo-73/noticias/2012-11-27/152210.html);
9 companies took part that year. In 2012 Cybermonday
[meant 25 million dollars for Chilean online retail](http://www.df.cl/cyber-monday-chile-preve-duplicar-su-oferta-de-marcas-participantes-en-la-version-2013/prontus_df/2013-11-17/200734.html).

Not bad for a single day.

Let's recap: Cybermonday 2012, 9 companies, sales of 25 million
dollars, 73 complaints.

> Every other day (every other day), every other day Every other day of
> the week is fine, yeah

This year is the third, and this time 26 companies took part
officially. One site, ABCDIN,
[reported receiving 600,000 visits in the first half hour](http://www.df.cl/pese-a-reclamos-sitios-superarian-las-expectativas-en-cybermonday-2013/prontus_df/2013-11-25/210902.html),
and apparently they ran quite well. That figure is 6 times last
year's, so here we have a case of a company that prepared and was
able to withstand the high demand. The same happened with Lan.com, as
reported in this piece from [Radio Bio Bio](http://www.biobiochile.cl/2013/11/25/frustracion-de-usuarios-por-colapso-marca-primeros-minutos-del-cyber-monday-2013.shtml)

Although the number of complaints
[went up compared to last year](http://www.biobiochile.cl/2013/11/25/cybermonday-chile-2013-acumula-33-denuncias-en-el-sernac.shtml),
we are talking about 100 complaints, against 73 in 2012.

The sites that have drawn the most criticism are Falabella and Sodimac,
which, despite having announced bigger investments and more preparation,
were apparently the ones that failed the most. Other problems
reportedly occurred on payment sites such as WebPay.

> But whenever monday comes, but whenever monday comes You can find me
> crying all of the time

So, can we say Cybermonday was a disaster?
[Can we doubt whether Chile is ready for something like CyberMonday?](http://noticias.terra.cl/tecnologia/bits-ciencia-sociedad/blog)
Can we say nothing has been learned, and that
[we are repeating a story of failure](http://www.andresbustamante.cl/2013/03/ciber-dumbday-dia-marmota/)?

With 100 complaints? Hard to argue. Don't get me wrong, this goes beyond the
technology and the availability of the website. There are the
logistics, the quality of the products, prices that are
actually deals, etc. But I want to discuss the technological
capacity to run an event like this. It is with the criticism on
that front, the strictly technical one, that I don't fully
agree. I don't think the results are as bad as they have been
perceived to be.

I think Chile is ready for something like Cybermonday, that the
technical capabilities are there. There are companies that have shown they can
put together a technological solution adequate to this peak in
demand: there are the cases of ABCDIN and Lan.com. I am not saying they
had zero failures, they surely had some, but they were able to
operate and scale up their capacity to serve.

The other cases mentioned, which draw the most attention because of
the size of the companies behind them, are the ones that have
raised the alarm. There is a lot to unpack here. These are cases where
we are probably looking at a software architecture
unsuited to this kind of situation, a classic example of
lasagna-style architectures and an apparent lack of technological innovation
that seems to be taking its toll. But that is material for another post. I prefer
not to judge, since shaking up certain paradigms, or
redirecting investment, often requires very big changes, a lot of political
maneuvering and a pile of other factors that are not technological.

Besides, there are a number of developers in Chile who are capable of
building solutions with solid architectures to run something like
Cybermonday. I hope those who had failures this year manage
their kinetic intelligence better and turn to the people who know, and worry
less about covering their backsides by hiring and buying
[Acme-brand software](/en/blog/lnds/2012/04/17/on-the-intelligence-of-the-coyote/).

On the other hand, should we conclude that Cybermonday was a
disaster? By any measure, I think not. Despite the sea of
tears that is Twitter, remember that it does not reflect reality. In
the coming days we will know the sales figures, and I assure you that
next year there will be another Cybermonday and it will go better than this year's, and
there will probably be more companies that rise to the occasion, but we will
[keep on complaining](/en/blog/lnds/2012/03/15/philoctetes/) all the same.
