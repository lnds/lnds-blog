Several people have asked me, on different occasions, about books or
readings to dig deeper into topics related to "the nature of software".

My [book](http://www.lnds.net/books) includes an appendix with a list of
recommended reading. Below I transcribe that chapter as a reading guide;
I've added Amazon links where the books are available on that platform.

# Recommended reading

Much of this book comes from my research over eight years of writing my
blog. Each article was inspired by some piece of news I saw on the web,
by some article I read in a specialized journal (like [Communications of The ACM](http://cacm.acm.org/), or
by some book I stumbled upon.

What follows are some readings that inspired, were a source of
information for, or complement each of the parts that make up this
volume.

## Part one: Origins {#primeraparteorgenes}

James Gleick's book
**["The Information: A History, a Theory, a Flood"](http://amzn.to/OWa7Co)** contains many details on the
origins of information theory and the development of computers. Much of
the story of Babbage and Ada Lovelace comes from there. I came across the
story of the Talking Drums in that book. It is one of the best books I
know on the history of computing.

**["Turing's Cathedral: The Origins of the Digital Universe"](http://amzn.to/1iqpdPF)**, by George Dyson. The author is the son
of Freeman Dyson, another great physicist and mathematician. As a child George Dyson got to know
Von Neumann and several people who worked at the Institute for Advanced
Study in Princeton. This book is a history of the development of the
first computers in the United States, under the strong influence of John
von Neumann.

**["Alan Turing: The Enigma, The Centenary Edition"](http://amzn.to/1kibRTT)**, by Andrew Hodges, the most
complete biography of the great English mathematician. This book is the
essential reference for learning about the fascinating life of this
genius.

**["Logicomix: An Epic Search of Truth"](http://amzn.to/1rr2OmA)**, a
brilliant comic written by Apostolos Doxiadis and Christos Papadimitriou.
This comic tells the story of the mathematicians of the early 20th
century and their efforts to solve Hilbert's program. Christos
Papadimitriou is the coauthor of Bill Gates's paper on pancake sorting.

**["Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!"](http://amzn.to/1iqMOK0)** This
autobiography of the great American physicist and Nobel laureate in
Physics is not only funny but very revealing, full of anecdotes that take
place during the development of the atomic bomb, in the famous Manhattan
Project.

**["The Code Book"](http://amzn.to/1lWvi48)**, by Simon Singh, a history
of cryptography, which describes the story of the Enigma machine and
Turing's work to break it.

"Memorandum For Members and Affiliates of the Intergalactic Computer
Network", the full text written by Licklider, is available online
<http://www.kurzweilai.net/memorandum-for-members-and-affiliates-of-the-intergalactic-computer-network>.

## Part two: Complexity 

**"Historia de las Matemáticas"**, by E.T. Bell, an old volume from
Fondo de Cultura Económica; I first read the stories of Archimedes,
Euclid, and Mersenne in that book.

**["Chaos: Making a New Science"](http://amzn.to/N80TS1)**, another book
by James Gleick. A best seller that explains things like fractals,
dynamical systems and, of course, complexity.

**["Meta Math! The Quest for Omega"](Meta%20Math!%20The%20Quest%20for%20Omega)**, by Gregory Chaitin,
for anyone interested in going deeper into this mathematician's ideas
and his particular epistemological vision.

**["The Annotated Turing: A guided Tour Through Alan Turing's Historic Paper on Computability and the Turing Machine"](http://amzn.to/R4Qnvm)**, by Charles Petzold. This author became
famous in the '90s for writing books on programming for the Windows
platform. In this book he explains Alan Turing's original paper step by
step, a titanic and remarkable piece of work, and well done in my
opinion.

**["Gödel's Proof"](http://amzn.to/1fhZ4lD)**, by Ernest Nagel, James
R. Newman and Douglas Hofstadter, which tries to give an explanation in
more accessible terms of Gödel's work and his incompleteness theorems.

**["Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid"](http://amzn.to/1k8Idia)**, by Douglas Hofstadter. One of the
most beautiful books I have read on mathematics, music, art and
artificial intelligence.

**["The Emperor's New Mind"](http://amzn.to/1lWw7tZ)**, by Roger
Penrose. Written as a reply to Hofstadter's work, Penrose lays out many
of the concepts of complexity, along with an introduction to the deep
ideas of quantum mechanics, in order to answer whether it is possible to
build an artificial intelligence.

## Part three: Solutions 

**["Nine Algorithms That Changed the Future"](http://amzn.to/1kicGMt)**,
by John MacCormick and Chris Bishop, an excellent introduction to
relevant algorithms that have shaped information technology.

"The Pathologies of Big Data", Queue Magazine, available online
<http://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=1563874> Almost everything presented
on the problem of parallelizing comes from the original papers by Amdahl,
Gustafson and Shi, which can be accessed in the archives of the journal
Communications of the ACM.

The web page of Mark D. Hill, a researcher at the University of
Wisconsin, has a lot of material on the problem of parallelizing:
<http://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/>

Google's Map Reduce algorithm is available on a dedicated page
<http://research.google.com/archive/mapreduce.html>

My C++ program that computes the median of simulated census data is
available on GitHub at the following address:
<https://github.com/lnds/La-Naturaleza-del-Software/blob/master/censo.cpp>

I recommend reading and visiting the site of the journal Communications
of The ACM: <http://cacm.acm.org/>, the source of inspiration for several
articles.

**["Programming Pearls"](http://amzn.to/1lWwzby)**, by Jon Bentley, a
book where you can learn a lot about how to solve problems in computing.

## Part four: Method

The ideas of Alan Kay that appear in these articles come from the
interview in Queue Magazine, "A Conversation with Alan Kay"
<http://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=1039523>

The book **["Lean Architecture: for Agile Software Development"](http://amzn.to/185Jang)**, by James Coplien, has changed my view of agile development.

**["Peopleware"](http://amzn.to/1ruUiRv)**, by Tom DeMarco and Timothy
Lister. A classic on managing IT teams, indispensable.

A London Business School study titled "Innovative Potential: Men and
Women in Teams". Available from the website:
<http://www.london.edu/assets/documents/facultyandresearch Innovative/PotentialNOV_2007.pdf>

**["The Mythical Man Month"](http://amzn.to/PHEONR)**, by Fred Brooks,
a book that must not be forgotten. The second edition contains the famous
essay "No Silver Bullets".

**["Hackers and Painters: Big Ideas from the Computer Age"](http://amzn.to/1iqOnaH)**, by Paul Graham, a selection of
essays by this author, who has been another inspiration for writing this
book.

**["The Black Swan: Second Edition"](http://amzn.to/1iqOzqH)**, by
Nicholas Taleb, a fundamental book for understanding randomness.
