At a recent dinner, one of the guests, a manager at a company, asked me
why people who work in IT are so "special", and above all so
self-absorbed (another word used at the table was autistic, but for
reasons I'll keep to myself, and out of respect, I prefer not to use
that term).

I ventured an answer, which really just leaned on the cliché that IT
people are like that. The truth is I wasn't satisfied with what I said
at that table, because later I came to the conclusion that this manager
probably has a problem with how she selects her team.

In my jobs I have been in charge of software engineers. For four years I
was deputy development manager, and now I'm in charge of an entire
technology area. Then and now, I was responsible for choosing the people
I would work with.

Choosing your team is crucial. The first step is the selection
interviews. A couple of months ago I had to interview candidates to pick
my new team. Since it had been years since I'd done job interviews, I
decided to catch up and read something on the subject, like Joel
Spolsky's famous article: [The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](http://www.joelonsoftware.com/articles/fog0000000073.html)
and the reply (not as famous, but very necessary) from Steve Yegge: 
[Done, and Gets Things Smart](http://steve-yegge.blogspot.com/2008/06/done-and-gets-things-smart.html)

If you have to hire IT people, I think these articles are worth reading.

The problem is that both articles concentrate mainly on a single aspect:
the candidates' intelligence.

Spolsky opens his article with the cardinal criterion he uses to select
people, which, as Yegge points out, has become an aphorism: Smart and
Gets Things Done.

Yegge's criticism is that Spolsky's method gets off on the wrong foot. I
won't explain his reasons, I suggest you read the article, but Yegge's
principle is that you should hire people smarter than you.

Well, both positions are interesting, but if you concentrate only on
people's IQ, you can end up with the problems of the person who asked me
the question at that dinner.

Because she has problems with her team's performance, but on top of
that, she notices she has a bunch of self-absorbed nerds, and that
worries her too.

I think the problem is that this model of the software engineer, the
nerd, nice and quiet but very intelligent, is a mistake.

If you see that the engineers you hired hide in a corner whenever the
company holds a social activity, and don't interact with the rest of the
employees, let me tell you: you're in trouble.

Because a person who is too quiet, with zero social skills, is a problem
you need to solve, and soon.

Developing software is a social endeavor. You can't build good software
with a set of engineers who have zero social skills.

A person who only expresses their feelings through chat, email, or by
taking part in web forums is a one-dimensional person, and to develop
software you have to think in four dimensions.

Thinking back, my worst experiences with the people who have reported to
me came when I chose the self-absorbed type. Often they are unable to
express their real problems. When they deal with users they can't get
the necessary information out of them, or in other cases they behave
arrogantly. And they are a problem for you, as their boss: it is
frustrating to work with someone when you don't know what they think or
what motivates them.

I won't deny that we IT people have a "special" personality: somewhat
rigid, obsessed with precision. Still, the good software engineer, in my
opinion, is the one who is flexible and knows how to communicate with
users, and for that you need normal social skills.

The self-absorbed one may be a coding genius, but without social skills
he will become an obstacle for the team and for its leader.

I'm not going to give you advice on how to pick people; more qualified
people have written about that, like the ones I already mentioned. The
only thing I'll tell you is to use "the third eye", "the Force",
whatever you want to call it. The intuitive side matters. It's good to
pull that person away from technology, where they probably feel safest,
and ask them about other things: general culture, books, movies, what
the recipe for a good mojito is, whatever. The psychological report is
important, but trust your instinct. And remember that the evaluation
doesn't end with the initial interview. The technical part is easy to
measure, but the social side gets evaluated in social activities.

I knew someone who worked with young people, and one of the first things
he did was invite them over to his house to play "Ataque" (a Risk-style
board game). It was his way of getting to know the kids' personalities.
It's unlikely you'll get together to play Ataque with your team (though
it wouldn't hurt to try), but there are plenty of opportunities to
assess their social profile: a lunch, a happy hour, or a company outing.

Don't make the mistake of choosing people based only on technical
criteria or IQ. It is always better to choose normal people than
self-absorbed geniuses.
