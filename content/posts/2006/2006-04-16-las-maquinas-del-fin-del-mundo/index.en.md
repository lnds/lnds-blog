I read with astonishment a post by my brother about a chain letter that [announces
the end of the world for this June 6 of this year
(6/6/6)](http://replay.waybackmachine.org/20071029052925/http://www.ricardodiaz.org/archives/2006/04/fin.html).

Apart from being an interesting numerical coincidence, what reasons are there
to think the world has to end?

## The end, courtesy of technology

The truth is that the end of the world has been announced many times, and not
only by religious fanatics.

Apocalyptic visions usually announce all sorts of
cataclysms, but, as you would expect, in recent times other
causes for the end of the world have been added, attributable to technology:

-   The end of the world through the use of biochemical weapons

-   All-out nuclear war

-   Nuclear plants blowing up

-   A super machine that creates a vortex, or a black hole

-   Global warming and a new ice age

-   The rise of artificial intelligences that destroy their
    creator

This last "threat" gives me the excuse to start an exploration
of artificial intelligence.

The fear of the rise of the machines has been discussed in box-office
hits like Terminator and The Matrix, and more subtly in "2001:
A Space Odyssey".

When people talk about this vision of the machine rising against its
creator, many compare it to Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. It's
actually not the same thing, since the "creature" is a human brought back to
life (well, a human built from the parts of many
corpses).

In The Matrix we don't know what the "machines" look like. We see squid-shaped
robots, but those are only "suits" to house artificial
intelligences. The artificial intelligences live in the matrix, a
virtual world with a physical substrate (hardware), fed by the
biochemical energy of millions of human beings.

In Terminator the idea is similar: an artificial intelligence emerges
from a military network (Skynet), and this intelligence decides to destroy
mankind. To do so it uses robots, known as terminators, but
once again, the AIs are somewhere else.

John Connor knows he must destroy Skynet before it takes shape, because
getting to Skynet is probably impossible. Neo heads toward a
sort of core of the Matrix, because he knows that even if they kill all the
squid robots, those aren't the problem.

The unseen enemies are Artificial Intelligences that live,
float, so to speak, in a network or physical substrate. They are
programs, in a way, yes, but programs with the ability
to learn and to manipulate their environment in order to protect themselves, for
example, by building robots.

The robots aren't the dangerous ones, they are tools. It's the
artificial intelligences that are in command, and they are the dangerous ones.

But, by their nature, they are elusive. In 2001, HAL is eliminated,
because its physical substrate is easy to get to. But in The Matrix and
Terminator that isn't possible, and the heroes of those sagas must try
to keep them from emerging, in the case of Terminator, or attack the core where
they reside, as in The Matrix.

However, John Connor's strategy is smarter than Neo's,
because John Connor wants to keep Skynet from ever existing, but that
produces an interesting paradox: if John Connor destroys Skynet, he
will cease to exist. In the case of The Matrix, we know Neo can't
destroy the AIs, that the fight is eternal and will repeat forever,
because Neo is, in a way, a mechanism of the matrix itself for
reorganizing itself.

Maybe that's why The Matrix disappoints so much as a trilogy. Because in the end
we feel betrayed. We expected a more exciting finale, but
they actually lead us to a disappointing ending in which the AIs win. We have
seen that only in 2001 does the hero manage to eliminate the threat of
the AI. HAL fails to protect itself.

We know that before building an AI we must build lower
levels of intelligence, and those rudiments of intelligence are what
we have today. These rudiments make up the
"instincts" of AIs. Without those basic instincts, which allow
the AI to develop, it won't be possible for it to become conscious.

An AI should

1.  Preserve itself

2.  Reproduce

3.  Evolve

These 3 basic requirements are the foundation of the so-called strong
thesis of artificial life. In essence it says that life is an
operational model, and if it satisfies the 3 operations mentioned, then
the physical substrate of that life doesn't matter.

The satisfaction of these 3 needs is what would justify the AIs'
hostility toward us. Indeed, if we make their
preservation precarious, if they have to compete with us for resources, or
we are a nuisance in the way of their evolution, the AIs may turn against
their creators.
