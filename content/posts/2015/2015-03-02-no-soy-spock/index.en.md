> "Change is the essential process of all existence" -- Spock

In 1975 the actor Leonard Nimoy published his first autobiography. The
book took its title from one of its chapters: "**I Am Not Spock**".
The phrase comes from an incident at an airport. On running into the
actor, a mother tells her daughter that the man in front of them is Spock.
The girl didn't look very convinced, so Nimoy explains that he isn't
really Spock, just the actor who played the character.
From that anecdote Nimoy builds a text about how
he came to develop the character.

Nowhere in the book does Nimoy disown the character; he even says that
given the chance to play any fictional character he would have
picked Spock without hesitation. In time, Nimoy
[admitted it was a mistake to pick that title for the book](http://www.ew.com/article/1995/09/22/search-spock). As usually
happens, nobody read the book or bothered to check anything, and the
myth was born that the actor despised Spock as a character. The mistake
keeps being repeated to this day, after the actor's
death.

In 1995 Leonard Nimoy wrote a second volume of his autobiography,
titled **"I Am Spock"**. The foreword of that book is a
letter from Spock to Leonard Nimoy expressing his confusion at how
illogical the title of the first part was. In this book Nimoy repeats that
Spock has always been a part of him, as an actor and as a
person.

But it doesn't matter. Very few people have probably read his
autobiography, and the obituaries will tell over and over how Nimoy had
mixed feelings about the character of
Spock.

The end of my vacation caught me off guard with the sad news of the
death of the veteran actor and film director. My son sent me a
WhatsApp message, with a sad face: "spock died :(".

Those who know me know I'm a
"[Trekkie](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek_fandom)", one of
those fans who has watched many times over the series created by Gene
Roddenberry, who imagined a future world where science and
technology have eliminated most of human greed,
where replicators can hand any food to
any person just for the asking, where computers converse
with people looking for solutions to transcendental problems, where
exploring new worlds and the mysteries of the universe is the
main motivation of the species. Where wars are over
because we have reached a high degree of maturity as a society. A
techno-socialist utopia, as some have labeled it.

All of us Star Trek fans are dreamers. We would like to be
able to visit strange new worlds, to stop
worrying about money, debt, poverty, hunger and
war, and devote ourselves to fulfilling our dreams as people.

If you hear Star Trek's optimistic message at a very
early age, and you are able to take it in, it's impossible not to be
moved by it.

But if you are a kid with deeper concerns, one who questions his
father's beliefs ("if God is love, why does he allow
suffering in the world?"), then not only does the message get
stronger, the character of Spock becomes a role
model.

In the episode "Dagger of the Mind", Spock and McCoy have the
following exchange:

> Mr. Spock: Interesting. You Earth people glorify organized violence
> for forty centuries, but you imprison those who employ
> it privately.\
> Dr. McCoy: And, of course, your people found an answer.\
> Mr. Spock: We disposed of emotion, Doctor. Where there's no emotion, there's no
> motive for violence.

I suppose that shaped the way I saw the world as a teenager,
because for a long time I really did repress my emotions and considered
Spock a role model.

In "Plato's Stepchildren", an episode that went down in history because
it features the first interracial kiss in the history of
American television, there is this other exchange:

> Dr. McCoy: The release of emotions, Mr. Spock, is what keeps
> us healthy, emotionally healthy, that is.\
> Mr. Spock: That may be, Doctor. However, I have noted that the
> healthy release of emotion is frequently very unhealthy for those
> closest to you.

These exchanges cemented the image of the Vulcans: peaceful
but serious people who try to remove all emotion from their lives, a
harder task for Spock, because being half human he had emotions,
just like the rest of us, which made you identify with the character even more.

But Mr. Spock himself gives the answer in the 1991 film "Star
Trek VI: The Undiscovered Country":

> "Logic is the beginning of wisdom, not the end."

In the process of growing up and maturing I learned this.

Emotions are part of you, as much as logic is. You have to learn to
express them, even the most violent ones, because otherwise you can get
seriously ill. Depression, which has affected me too, has its
origin in repressed anger. In time I learned to express my anger,
much more than I used to, and I overdid it. I became violent and
irritable.

Learning to calibrate my feelings, to find a balance, is still
a permanent quest in my life. I can't deny that Mr.
Spock was a big influence on my personal development, but
fortunately I grew up, matured and discovered that I am not Spock, that nobody can
be like Spock, really.

Nimoy's great achievement was to embody this very strange character, one we could
only conceive of in an extraterrestrial. But don't try to take him as
a model of personality; that's childish. The best part of being human is
the emotions, the good ones and the bad ones. We are what we are thanks to
them, and trying to repress them is not good.

What we should take from the character is his wisdom and his ability to look for
peaceful solutions to problems. Sometimes it's good to step back,
think with a cooler head and [use logic](/blog/lnds/2010/7/17/logico-y-razonable),
which is nothing more than looking for the most reasonable solution.

This post is to express my affection for the character and for the man who played him, the
great Leonard Nimoy. His death made me sad but, as Spock would say, it is
the logical consequence of his age and state of health :-).

I just wanted to write this to say goodbye. Goodbye Leonard Nimoy, goodbye
Spock, "I have been, and always shall be, your
friend".
