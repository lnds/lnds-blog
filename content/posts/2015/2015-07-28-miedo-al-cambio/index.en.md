I don't like going down stairs. Let me be more precise: I don't like going
down stairs fast.

When I was a kid I tumbled down a staircase, and it was painful and traumatic. I remember
going to the doctor, and for some reason I remember seeing
my black eyes for days. Since then I don't dare go down
stairs fast, even less if they have no
handrail.

***"Fear is the enemy of
speed"***. Only
when we overcome fear can we go
faster.

Eric Elliott argues in [this article](//medium.com/javascript-scene/how-to-build-a-high-velocity-development-team-4b2360d34021)
that eliminating fear is the most important factor in
[building a high velocity development team](//medium.com/javascript-scene/how-to-build-a-high-velocity-development-team-4b2360d34021).

Every time we are about to go to production we are scared to
death:

What if a critical bug slipped past us?

Or if the user hates the new experience?

And what if the production servers fail?

They are all fears, legitimate or not, plain fears of change.

Change is scary, and our organization is afraid of change.

The role of a development lead, according to Elliott, is to eradicate the fear of
change. We have to get to the point where nobody is afraid to change a line of
code.

The big problem we have is that development teams are
afraid of change, and that is what makes them slow.

They are like me with stairs. I'm sure an eighty-year-old
lady would get down a staircase faster than I do, and that's embarrassing.

The same goes for development: we have to be able to make changes day
after day, not once, ideally several times a day.

Ask yourself these questions that Eric Elliott proposes:

1. How long does it take your engineering team to fix a bug and deploy
the fix to production?

2. Has your team ever done a *"hot fix"*
of the code in production? Has that ever failed?

3. Can your team prototype and test a major redesign of the
user interface on your live site, without affecting the experience of
all your users?

4. Have you waited weeks or months for simple changes to be
pushed live to your customers?

As Dory said in Finding Nemo, "Well, you can't never let anything happen to him.
Then nothing would ever happen to him". It's the same with your development team:
if it never faces one of the four scenarios above,
it will never become a high performance, high
velocity team.

Change has to become so routine that nobody worries about
it happening anymore, and changes only happen when we stop fearing them.
