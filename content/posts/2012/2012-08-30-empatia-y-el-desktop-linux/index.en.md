In answering the question of [what killed the Linux desktop?](http://tirania.org/blog/archive/2012/Aug-29.html) Miguel de
Icaza proposes two dimensions: (a) things change too fast,
breaking both open source and proprietary software, (b)
incompatibility across Linux distributions.

Miguel de Icaza's claim is a loaded one, because it declares dead something
that some question was ever alive, and that for others is still
not a settled matter. But the truth is that many developers who
ran Linux as their desktop have switched to Mac OSX. Why?

Because the curious thing is that Mac OSX, with all its virtues in terms
of usability, hardware integration, and integration
with the other devices in its ecosystem, is a trap in terms of
freedom, at least as it is understood by many of the more fundamentalist hackers
who defend free software (the ones who like to talk about
Gnu/Linux, for example).

I think the Linux desktop's problem has its root in a
phenomenon that has to do with empathy, or to put it in more
cartoonish terms, that sort of "aspergerization of technology"[^1]
we fall into in computing. Zero empathy for the user; what
matters is the intellectual fun, architecture for
architecture's sake, technology for technology's sake.

Architecture is about thinking through the environments that human
beings will use, and to develop an architecture you need to have a
notion of what the human needs are, and one basic need is
to have a degree of security and stability, along with the need for
the architecture to cover the basics, so that it doesn't force us to
worry about things that should be taken care of.

The key is in these paragraphs of what de Icaza says:

> "As for myself, I had fallen in love with the iPhone, so
> using a Mac on a day-to-day basis was a must. Having been
> part of the Linux Desktop efforts, I felt a deep guilt for
> liking OSX and moving a lot of my work to it.

Backwards compatibility, and compatibility across Linux
distributions is not a sexy problem. It is not even remotely
an interesting problem to solve. Nobody wants to do that
work, everyone wants to innovate, and be responsible for the
next big feature in Linux.\
So Linux was left with idealists that wanted to design the
best possible system without having to worry about boring
details like support and backwards compatibility.

Meanwhile, you can still run the 2001 Photoshop that came
when XP was launched on Windows 8. And you can still run your
old OSX apps on Mountain Lion.

Back in February I attended FOSDEM and two of my very dear
friends were giggling out of excitement at their plans to roll
out a new system that will force many apps to be modified to
continue running. They have a beautiful vision to solve a
problem that I never knew we had, and that no end user
probably cares about, but every Linux desktop user will pay
the price.

That day I stopped feeling guilty about my new found love
for OSX."

What Miguel de Icaza is telling us is that the people working on the
Linux desktop are not interested in what it costs the
end user to make the changes they have to make to their environment,
because what they care about is chasing a technological ideal that has little
to do with the user's needs. Empathy: none.

[^1]: I don't mean to offend anyone. What I'm referring to is that a
sort of myth has grown up around Asperger's, to the point of believing
that it is a good thing to be inconsiderate or socially maladjusted as long as
you are intelligent, or that a person's lack of empathy gets excused because after
all it is a "condition", when I suspect that in many cases
people simply abuse the term so that we'll put up with them.
