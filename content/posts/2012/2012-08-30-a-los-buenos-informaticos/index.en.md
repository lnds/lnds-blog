To the good IT people {#alosbuenosinformticos}
=========================

by
[aldrinmartoq](https://aldrinmartoq.wordpress.com/author/aldrinmartoq/)

When I met Eduardo in person I asked him if he played any
instrument. I usually do this because I have a theory that there is a
significant correlation between IT person and frustrated musician. After
thinking for a bit, he answered that yes, when he was younger he played
the drums.

We also talked about how a friend and I saw software development
as very similar to making a movie. Eduardo told me he thought
exactly the same and was somewhat amazed to find someone with
the same idea. Later, talking with my friend, we realized it was
no simple coincidence: he had read this blog (to be precise:
[Developing software is like making a
movie](/blog/2005/08/desarrollar-software-es-como-hacer-una-pelicula.html))
and that is why we had discussed the subject.

It is a great honor for me to take part in the anniversary of this blog which,
as you can see, has reached us directly or indirectly through all these
years.

Poorly focused IT people
==========================

Something I repeat constantly is that the main problem with
IT people is the tremendous lack of focus in everything we do.

It is something we see day after day: we build applications that are far from
solving the original requirement; we work on problems that are not
aligned with the solution; we look for answers to the eternal suffering
of getting any system up and running by reinventing new methodologies.

This happens for many reasons.

Often we get so deep into a problem that we completely
forget why we are doing what we are doing.
We end up absorbed in trying to connect to the Web Service;
we fight not to mix the data model with view logic
(because that's what best practices say); or we are very
worried about getting the cluster working with the disaster recovery site... And
we forget that the application doesn't run forward or backward, let
alone in parallel, something I think we are far from understanding well.

Or we copy formulas that apply to other disciplines. If it worked for
Toyota, why not for us? The whole design patterns thing comes
from architecture and other models that apply to physical things, but
computing is something so abstract that we can't just show up and apply those
formulas to the magical thing that software is. Analogies are good, but
they have a scope. And a limit, like any model.

The problem for me remains the same: if we focused on
solving problems in their context we would realize this and
much more.

For me the best example of focus is Apple. Many people label me a
*fanboy* when I actually find that OS X in general leaves quite a lot to
be desired, for example. It is horrendously slow because it is not a native BSD
but is mounted on top of something else (Mach); the file system
does some pretty strange things; the ridiculous amount of resources it
consumes is disgusting; etc.

Did you know that the graphical interface in OS X is not 100% optimized?
There is a technology called QuartzGL that after 7 years still ships
disabled and is only available through a utility called Quartz
Debug.

[![](https://aldrinmartoq.files.wordpress.com/2012/08/2012-08-30-01-59-13-am.png?w=584)](https://aldrinmartoq.files.wordpress.com/2012/08/2012-08-30-01-59-13-am.png)

If this were Linux they would already have rewritten all the drivers and changed the
GTK API to implement something similar to QuartzGL or whatever. But
Apple understands that there are more important things for its users than
100% fluid graphics. After all, the system is not perfect but it meets
its goals quite well and there are more important problems to
solve. That is focus.

Has it ever happened to you that a plumber comes to fix a faucet and at
the end of the day you discover there is no hot water? That is IT
today. And the excuses are similar: "No, I already fixed the shutoff valve
under the sink, what's the problem?". "Ah, but look, the hot
water had the same problem and you didn't tell me anything".

Well, many may feel offended at being compared to a
two-bit plumber. But that is the second part of this article.

Slave labor 
===================

To give you a possible reason for our lack of focus, I'll tell you
a story.

The other day a friend came into the office while I was programming and saw my
screen full of source code. She said: "How booooooring, Aldrin! Doesn't
it bore you to look at that screen? It looks like The Matrix". I answered: "No,
not at all! In fact, doing this is what I like". "Mmm, very good!"
she replied.

[![](https://aldrinmartoq.files.wordpress.com/2012/08/2012-08-29-06-45-58-pm.png?w=300&h=179)](https://aldrinmartoq.files.wordpress.com/2012/08/2012-08-29-06-45-58-pm.png)

The Greeks had the same disgust for this kind of work... how
horrible, those people who "get their hands dirty" and work on "machine
things". Invention and experimentation were manual labor, something
not worthy of men. Those who did what today we
would call engineering were non-humans, that is, slaves.

Does it ring a bell, does it feel familiar, that sense that we are working
without dignity, just like slaves?

This same feeling is somewhat tragicomic and creates contradictions we see
day after day. A couple of days ago my friend (the same
one) appeared in a feature on the evening news. But not for something related to
IT; it was because he goes to a theater workshop. The feature was
longer: there were people who had always wanted to be soccer players, some girls who
wanted to be models, etc. Guess what: almost all the people interviewed
were IT people.

It seems nobody wants to be an IT person anymore. My initial hunch that
software engineer ≈ frustrated musician is going from bad to worse: I have
run into filmmakers, cooks, actors and a long etcetera. All of them
frustrated. Plainly, nobody declares themselves a programmer anymore, as Eduardo
complains. And this is fatal: I think understanding programming and having the
experience of it is fundamental to what we do.

The fact that nobody labels themselves a programmer anymore, but rather a geek, brogrammer
or developer, is a good indicator. It is striking that many
end the workday with the same sense of release as a slave:
at last this degrading day of work is over.

I feel that a good part of the root of the lack of focus comes from the fact that
we are not happy with, or fulfilled by, what we are doing. We are a
mass of frustrated-whatevers instead of being plainly IT people.

And you: are you a frustrated IT person?
