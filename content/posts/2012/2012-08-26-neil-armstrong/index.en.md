I was a kid of 6 or 7 when my father would screen films for us about
the Apollo 11 landing on the moon. He had an old film
projector, with copies of newsreels about the 1962 World Cup and the
NASA briefing films on the space program, which he got from the
Instituto Chileno Norteamericano de Cultura in Chuquicamata. He also
had some LIFE magazines about the space race, and a series of
books the same magazine published in the late 1960s and early
1970s.

With all that influence it was only natural that I wanted to be an astronaut, like
John Glenn, Alan Shepard or Neil Armstrong himself. I read a lot
about these astronauts' stories as a kid, and even if it sounds
nerdy I'm a fan of movies like Apollo 13 or The Right Stuff (oh, I'm
a huge fan of that one, when I take the time to set up the home
theater gear to enjoy the sound of that film).

So when Neil Armstrong came to Chile I was there and I listened to him. He
struck me as a very simple man, an ordinary gentleman recounting his
great feat. I remember two moments from that talk. The first is when
he describes his impression on approaching the moon and noticing that it is a sphere, a
three-dimensional object, something we can't appreciate from the surface
of the earth. The other moment was more moving: when he recounts the
first step on the moon. At that instant all of us in the audience got
to our feet to applaud him.

It's true, much of the trip to the moon has to do with one more battle
in the context of the Cold War; there are even skeptics who
ridiculously believe the whole thing was staged. We forget how
remarkable this feat was, how risky it was. If we consider
that what separated these men from the cold of space and from
radiation, in some parts of the spacecraft, was nothing more than a sheet of
aluminum foil\...

It was a great feat carried out by brave men, the kind who
awaken admiration in a six-year-old boy, an admiration that
will stay in his heart forever\....
