In the play Breaking the Code, written by the
British playwright Hugh Whitemore about the life of Alan Turing, there is
a scene depicting the interview Dillwyn Knox gives him
before recruiting him to the Bletchley Park team to break the
Enigma codes.

In that scene Knox asks him to explain his theoretical work
in mathematics in simple terms. Turing answers:

> "It's a technical paper in mathematical logic, but it's also about
> the difficulty of telling right from wrong. People
> think --- most people think --- that in mathematics we always know
> what is right and what is wrong. Not so. Not any more. It's a problem
> that's occupied mathematicians for forty or fifty years. How can you tell
> right from wrong?"

Think about the Pinocchio paradox:

![](paradojadepinocho2.png)

If Pinocchio says his nose will grow, and it doesn't, he would be
lying, but when Pinocchio lies his nose grows, and if his nose
grows then he would be telling the truth. So what the hell is supposed to
happen after Pinocchio says that sentence?

This is a restatement of the old 
[Liar's Paradox](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradoja_del_mentiroso). The
interesting thing is that this paradox plays a fundamental role in the
solution of an important problem in mathematical logic, one that
eventually led to the development of Turing's ideas and his famous machine
(and without it there would be no computers, no web, no blog, no Twitter,
and we would not be telling this story this way).

At the beginning of the twentieth century mathematicians were trying to build
the foundations of mathematics, to turn it into the most beautiful and
purest of the sciences. On these foundations of solid rock the other,
more complex areas of mathematics would rest, and expand.
Eventually every proposition in mathematics could be
declared, uniquely and crystal clear, either false or true, and whenever
a mathematician proved a theorem little bells would ring in heaven and
an angel would get its wings. How lovely!

One of the greatest works embodying this ideal was that of
Whitehead and Russell, set down in their three-volume
magnum opus [Principia Mathematica](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_mathematica). 

The tremendous effort of these two thinkers, which took them years, reaches
its peak when on page 379 of the first volume the
proof that 1+1 = 2 is set up, and it is finally completed on page 86
of the second volume! (thank goodness)

Back then there was a great mathematician named Hilbert who posed a
series of problems to his fellow mathematicians. It is suspected that after
falling asleep reading the Principia he decided to have other enthusiasts
summarize the three heavy volumes for him, and he posed what is known by the
grandiose name of **[Hilbert's second problem](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_second_problem)**, which
in plain terms can be translated as: *"can somebody explain to me
whether this s**\*** by Whitehead and Russell is consistent!?"*

That is, what Hilbert wanted was for some pimply thesis student to
tell him whether those three bricks full of mathematical gibberish
contained internal contradictions.

And good old Kurt Gödel solved the problem, and found that the
answer was 42..... nooo, just kidding, the answer to Hilbert's second
problem is negative.

Gödel, considered the greatest logician of the twentieth century, whom we see in
the photo out for a walk with one of his
buddies, was
quite a character. 

{{<figure caption="Einstein and Gödel" src="einstein_godel.jpg">}}

The story goes that his scientist friends
got him asylum in the United States to get him out of Austria and away from
the Nazi regime. In time they convinced him to accept
American citizenship. Rigorous as he was, he decided to carry out a
logical analysis of the Constitution of the United States, and discovered
that without stepping outside the constitution it was possible to establish a
fascist-style dictatorship in that country (how odd, don't you think?). Worried,
he told his friends that he refused to swear allegiance to this country under those
conditions, unless some changes were made to the
constitution. It took some convincing, but they managed to restore his confidence
and he agreed to swear loyalty to the American constitution, a
prerequisite for being granted citizenship. Well, the official who
was to administer the oath asked him where he was from, and on hearing his answer the
clerk remarked how good it was to be in a nation where
democracy protected them from the rise of a tyranny. Big
mistake! On hearing this, poor Gödel jumped as if stung and started
explaining that he was wrong, and that the constitution had a series of
logical flaws, and at that point his friends had to step in,
cover his mouth and calm him down so the ceremony could go on.

Well, before that anecdote took place, and while still a shy thesis student,
Kurt Gödel proved his famous incompleteness theorems. And to do it he
used a variant of the liar's paradox. Basically what
Gödel did was formulate a proposition that says "This proposition is
unprovable". If the proposition is unprovable, then it is true,
and therefore the formulation of arithmetic is incomplete (because
there is an unproven proposition); on the other hand, if the proposition
can be proved, that is, it is false, then the formulation of the
theory is inconsistent.

The interesting thing is that Gödel's proof involves building
a sort of logical machine, a rudimentary computer that writes
logical statements following certain rules, which makes Gödel's
original proof long and intricate.

Today Gödel's result covers much more than number
theory and arithmetic, thanks to the precise notion of a computational
algorithm introduced by Alan Turing. Nowadays Gödel's
theorem is considered applicable to every formal axiomatic system.

What matters to us, in summary, is that the work of Gödel, Turing
and other logicians and mathematicians of the early twentieth century, who set out to
solve these abstract fundamental problems of mathematics,
led to the development of the concepts of algorithms and of automatic
machines that resulted in the development of modern computing.

We will talk about Turing's achievement, which followed Gödel's feat, in
the second-to-last article of this series.
