The first programming language I learned was Basic, back around 1981,
on a Sinclair ZX81. Do you know what [Dijkstra](/blog/2009/07/calidad-correccion-y-elegancia-una-entrevista-a-dijkstra.html)
said about Basic? Dijkstra said: *"The teaching of BASIC should be
rated as a criminal offence: it mutilates the mind beyond recovery"*.

You'll understand my concern when I found out that one of the people I
admire most thought that way. Was my mind mutilated forever?

But then there's another of the great heroes of our field, Alan Kay, who
said: *"arrogance in computer science is measured in nano-Dijkstras"*
(to which someone replied "and in microkays").

It turns out that in the early 1970s Alan Kay was a researcher at the
University of Utah who had taken part in developing the
[Flex](http://www.mprove.de/diplom/gui/kay68.html) language. The idea
was to bring in the ideas of Simula and Sketchpad to develop graphical
interfaces for personal computers.

Alan Kay had this vision he called the DynaBook, a portable system with
a flat, touch-sensitive screen. A device that is the direct ancestor of
the notebook and the tablets. A machine to be used by adults and
children alike.

Alan Kay cared about education. In 1972, when he presented his
prototype of the [DynaBook](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynabook), at
MIT [Seymour Papert](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papert) and his team,
inspired by the educational ideas of [Jean
Piaget ](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Piaget), were designing the
Logo language. The key to Logo was its ability to create small building
blocks, which were later combined to build more complex structures.
These ideas inspired Alan Kay to develop SmallTalk, a language based
entirely on the notions of objects and message passing between them.

All of this was built at the Xerox labs in Palo Alto, the same ones
visited by Steve Jobs and his "pirates of Silicon Valley". Kay's ideas,
embodied in SmallTalk, changed user interfaces and have shaped the
development of personal computers ever since. While I write this on my
notebook, my wife is playing Sudoku and browsing the web on a tablet.
Our world has been shaped by his ideas.

The most interesting part of this whole story is that the first
SmallTalk interpreter had to be built in a language that could run in
real time, and efficiently, on the computers of the day. The first
SmallTalk interpreter was written in BASIC.
