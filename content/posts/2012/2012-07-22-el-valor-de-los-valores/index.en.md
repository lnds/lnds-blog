Here's an exercise for tomorrow, when you get back to work: look at
your company's values, read them, explore what they mean. Ask
your coworkers what they mean, or your bosses. Or maybe something more basic:
go find your company's values. They should be in some document, or
in a nice frame hanging in a human resources office, right?

When those values are there, hanging on an office wall or on the
corporate web page, we find texts written in the passive voice,
full of stock phrases and well-meaning, politically
correct words that seem to have no value at all. I'm not saying it
always happens, but I suspect the vast majority of these lists of corporate
values were written without much conviction, and it shows
when they aren't applied in the company's day-to-day.

There are all kinds of statements. There are the vague or generic ones:


> "..\[it is\] as important to achieve the economic and financial
> objectives and results as the way in which those
> results are obtained."

> "Beyond strict compliance with laws, codes of conduct and
> internal rules, all our professionals work with the utmost
> transparency and honesty, integrating into the performance of their work
> our institution's commitment to the economic, social
> and environmental progress of the communities where it is present."

Or the pompous and self-centered statements:


> *\*Leadership: \**Our calling is leadership in every
> market where we are present, relying on the best
> teams of people and being customer- and results-oriented\....

> **Being Better**: We contribute to our customers' success
> through the use of our products and services, to which end
> we commit a permanent deployment of innovation, excellence,
> quality of service\...

Or simply the lists of words:

-   Commitment

-   Service Attitude

-   Teamwork

-   Flexibility

The gringos have a wonderful word for all of that:

# **BULLSHIT!**

{{<figure src="bullshit.jpeg" caption="We want no more bullshit">}}

That's why I liked it so much when I read the values of
[Atlassian](http://www.atlassian.com/), which I transcribe
below:

### Open Company, No Bullshit {#opencompanynobullshit}

Atlassian embraces transparency wherever at all practical, and sometimes
where impractical. All information, both internal and external, is
public by default. We are not afraid of being honest with ourselves, our
staff and our customers.

### Build with Heart and Balance 

Everyday we try to build products that are useful and that people lust
after. Building with heart means really caring about what we\'re making
and doing--it\'s a mission, not just a job. When we build with balance
we take into account how initiatives and decisions will affect our
colleagues, customers and stakeholders.


### Don\'t \#@!% the Customer {#dontthecustomer}

When we make internal decisions we ask ourselves "how will this affect
our customers?" If the answer is that it would \'screw\' them, or make
life more difficult, then we need to find a better way. We want the
customer to respect us in the morning.

### Play, as a Team {#playasateam}

We want all Atlassians to feel like they work with Atlassian, not for
Atlassian. We think it\'s important to have fun with your workmates
while working and contributing to the Atlassian team.

### Be the Change You Seek {#bethechangeyouseek}

We think Gandhi had it pretty right when he said "We need to be the
change we wish to see in the world". At Atlassian we encourage everyone
to create positive change--we\'re constantly looking for ways to improve
our company, our products and our environment.

We could argue that it's just another way of presenting the same thing, but
no. Go and read [Atlassian's values
page](http://www.atlassian.com/company/about/values). There is
dedication there. You can tell they took the time to write, prepare and
present those values to all of us. There's even a video:

It's no coincidence that in a TED talk Daniel Pink uses this company
as an example of intrinsic motivation:

{{<ted "dan_pink_the_puzzle_of_motivation">}}

\
That is what we need in our companies, in our
workplaces: to make values truly have value, not just
to know them and spread them. It's about living them, accepting them,
understanding them, and wanting your whole team to feel them as part of who
they are, as their real motivation.

Sometimes these values aren't stated explicitly, but they are
lived by the leaders, by the people in charge of directing and motivating the
team. That, probably, matters more than stating them in a
nice frame.

Have you taken the time to think about your team's values, about
your company's values? Do they feel right to you, well
expressed, something that truly commits you? If not, then discuss them with
your bosses, your peers and your team, because having values that
commit us is the best way to have the job we want.
