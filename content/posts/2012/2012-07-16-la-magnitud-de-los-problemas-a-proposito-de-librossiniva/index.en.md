"A million dollars, a billion, a trillion, whatever. It doesn't matter as long
as we do something about the problem"[^1], but how do you
solve the problem if you don't know its magnitude?

I don't know if you're familiar with [Godwin's law](http://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ley_de_Godwin) about debates on
the Internet, the one that says:

> *"As an online discussion grows longer, the probability of
> a comparison involving Nazis or Hitler
> approaches one"[^2]*

The corollary was supplied by Richard Sexton:

> *"It follows that a USENET discussion has run its course when one of
> the participants mentions Hitler and the Nazis."*

In other words, once somebody brings up Hitler and Nazism the discussion no longer
makes sense. It has gone off the rails.

I want to propose a similar law, but for the discussion
about VAT on books (the #librossiniva, "books without VAT", campaign):

> "As the discussion about VAT on books in Chile grows
> longer, the probability that someone will botch the arithmetic
> approaches one."

This argument over VAT on books in Chile is ancient. I remember that
in the late 80s it was the writer Enrique Lafourcade's
battle cry. I suspect, and I'm sure there must be evidence, that
somebody back then made the same classic mistake: to calculate the
net price of the book, they subtracted the VAT percentage from 100. Online alone
I've seen it three times, and although I have the links I'm not going to post
them, to protect my readers from the horror :) [^3].

The point is very simple: if the book is worth 100, and VAT in Chile is
19, the amount the buyer pays, the one that shows up on the invoice or
receipt, is \$119.

The trouble comes when the calculation is done backwards. Since VAT is
19%, the reasoning leads them to subtract 19% from the invoice
amount. Let me show you the complicated inverse calculation that
almost always gets done:

Price of the book with VAT: 119\
19% of the price above: 22.61\
Price of the book without VAT: 119 - 22.61 = 96.39

And with that they have magically knocked 3.61 pesos off a book that cost \$
100.\[4\]

Believe it or not, I know plenty of educated people, with university degrees,
who do this same wrong calculation (including engineers,
sadly).

When Luzifer (Mario Waissbluth) [answers Jodorowsky's
questionnaire](http://www.papeldigital.info/elsemanal/2012/07/08/01/paginas/022.pdf)
and is asked what miracle he would like to witness, he replies
that he would like to see the day when every graduate of
Latin America's schools understands what they read and knows how to calculate a percentage.

Now, someone will argue that this is irrelevant, that 3 pesos more
or 3 pesos less don't matter as long as the problem of VAT on
books gets solved. The truth is that it does matter.

I insist: how can you solve a problem if the people debating it
have no idea of its magnitude?

And that happens with many public debates in our country (and in every
country). It happens with the debate over the minimum wage, the
profitability of the isapres (the private health insurers), the nationalization of lithium, or energy
savings. Again and again arguments are brandished that rest on
sloppy arithmetic.

Numbers don't lie, right? As I showed a few years ago
(in the post [numerical deceptions](/en/blog/lnds/2009/08/23/numerical-deceptions/)), a
badly framed calculation can be the foundation for the ruin of a public
policy. What's more, numbers can be presented in such a way that they
make us take decisions that can be very costly (one example is
anchoring, which [I covered earlier](/en/blog/lnds/2011/07/05/irrational-decisions/)).

In general I'm in favor of there being no taxes at all. A VAT of
19% on anything is obscenely high. It is real oppression
against us, because we are the ones who pay it. If we are going to
fight to lower taxes, let's do it for real: get rid of VAT,
or lower it to more reasonable rates.  
[Why fool ourselves and lie to ourselves yet again](/en/blog/lnds/2012/06/30/lies/)?
Behind these campaigns is the desire not to have to file VAT.
There is no altruistic spirit and no wish for people to read more. That is the
plain truth.

Cutting VAT on books has always been presented as a
solution to the problem of how little people read in Chile. The argument goes that
people don't read because such a high VAT discourages buying books.
A simple look at the price of books in other countries reveals that
this argument isn't true. We can come up with many other reasons
that better explain why people don't buy books, from
habits to the inability to understand them. I think the explanation
is very simple: the book market is small and the money is in
textbooks and educational books, which people are forced to buy.

This small market leads publishers and booksellers to set high
margins (yes, sometimes over 100%). Look at the big
bookstores with their roomy, luxurious spaces in the rich neighborhoods of
our city. You are not paying just for the books. If
VAT on books were eliminated in Chile they would still be expensive,
and few people would keep buying them, I can assure you of that. Let's admit it:
in Chile books are not much appreciated.

But this kind of campaign [is a first step](http://www.material-ligero.cl/un-punto-de-partida-librossiniva/),
the argument goes. Well, it seems to me it would be better to take other steps first. Like
improving reading comprehension and promoting critical reasoning (logical
and arithmetical).

The day people know how to properly size up the magnitude of their
problems they will be able to face them properly, and that starts with knowing
how to handle numbers.

And don't give me stories about reader altruism.
