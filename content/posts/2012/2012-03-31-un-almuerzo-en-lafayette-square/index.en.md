In December 1942, the exact date is impossible to pin down, three men
met for lunch at the restaurant of the Hay-Adams hotel in Lafayette
Square, Washington D.C. They were not three politicians, or generals
worried about the course of World War II. They were three geniuses
thinking about the future.

Each of them had already made a great contribution to science. Although
we don't know what they talked about at that meeting, that afternoon
they probably laid the foundations for the development of the modern
digital computer, and for everything that came afterward and that we
know as information technology. The three men who met for lunch in
Lafayette Square were none other than the founding fathers of computing:
Alan Turing, Claude Shannon and John von Neumann.

{{<figure caption="John von Neumann" src="neumann-alumnos.jpg">}}

The first two to arrive at the restaurant were Turing and Shannon. Both
were working on secret cryptography projects at AT&T's laboratories. By
then Alan Turing had left the leadership of the Hut 8 group at Bletchley
Park, the famous home of the British government's Government Code and
Cypher School. Hut 8 was in charge of breaking the messages encrypted
with the Enigma machine used by the German navy. Alan Turing had
expressed his interest in working on the encryption of human speech, and
to that end he asked to visit Bell Labs. He and Shannon shared a table
there several times, and we have a record that on one occasion, at tea
time, Turing showed him his 1936 paper on what we now know as the
"Universal Turing Machine". Claude Shannon was of course impressed,
since many of the ideas laid out in that paper complemented his own. He
had already shown in his 1937 thesis that it was possible to express
statements of logic and arithmetic by combining relays and electrical
circuits.

Besides working together at Bell Labs, Turing and Shannon shared the
fact that, at different times, they had studied at Princeton's Institute
for Advanced Study, both under the tutelage of John von Neumann. When
von Neumann heard about Turing's visit, he arranged the get-together
with his two brilliant former students.

Dan Wohlbruck [imagines the scene](http://pragprog.com/magazines/2011-12/when-did-that-happen):

> Neither Turing nor Shannon had previously dined at the Hay-Adams and
> as they got out of the taxi cab they shared, they paused. Turing
> turned to look at the White House just across Lafayette Square.
> Shannon, a true American from the Midwest, had seen the White House
> but had never enjoyed the luxury of a restaurant like the Lafayette
> Room. A bit intimidated, Shannon waited for Turing to lead the way.
> The men were seated at the table von Neumann had reserved and waited
> for their host to arrive.

{{<figure caption="Alan Turing" src="Alan_Turing.jpg">}}

The two men couldn't have been more different. Turing was short and
well dressed. Shannon was tall, thin, and ill at ease in his clothes.
Apart from having been von Neumann's students, the only thing the two
men shared was their common project, and that was no help because they
weren't allowed to discuss it in public. So, with nothing else to talk
about, they swapped stories about Johnny.

John von Neumann was well known and admired in Washington. His
intelligence was obvious without being showy. He handed out his
expertise and wisdom without wanting favors in return. When he testified
before congressional committees, senators and representatives hung on
every phrase. He was trusted, believed, and known by everyone.

Suddenly the room fell silent, as all eyes turned to the man making his
way among the tables from one end of the restaurant to the other. Johnny
von Neumann had arrived. He stopped to greet Senator Truman and, farther
along, to say a word to a White House staffer. Without consciously
thinking about it, both Turing and Shannon stood up and waited by their
chairs as a show of respect. Von Neumann finally reached the table and
the three men shook hands with warm affection, then sat down and ordered
lunch.

Over coffee, once the lunch dishes had been cleared, von Neumann steered
his former students toward a problem he was facing.

{{<figure caption="Claude Shannon" src="shannon.jpeg">}}

What followed was something rare and possibly unique. These three
shared a different kind of genius. Turing's mind knew no limits. He
acted without prejudice or inhibition and could work out a solution
without worrying about conventional wisdom. Shannon, for his part, was
able to connect two different fields of study by finding how they
interacted. John von Neumann was an assimilator. Where Turing and
Shannon could explain what should be done, von Neumann could describe
how to do it.

"Alan," he said, "I need a machine that can reason. I want something
that I can feed with information, and to which I can then pose a
question, and wait for an answer."

Turing considered the challenge. After thinking for a moment he
suggested an approach. Shannon had an inspiration and added his own
insight. As the two men grew more excited, von Neumann took notes and
drew diagrams on a napkin. Forty minutes later, it was done.

As a final flourish, Turing said, "And thus a machine can think."

"What, I wonder, would such a machine think about?" von Neumann
mused\...

Dan Wohlbruck's account is fiction. The meeting of these three men did
take place at that hotel in December 1942, and we cannot know what they
talked about, but it is very likely that they discussed how to build a
general-purpose digital computer. We know that von Neumann put his idea
into an important document describing the architecture of computers
with programs stored in memory, and that he worked on the EDVAC project
and on the famous ENIAC, the first general-purpose electronic computer.
Turing knew Colossus, the secret computer developed by the British to
break the German codes, and in later years he also worked on the
Manchester Mark 1.

Practically every modern computer implements the Turing machine model,
is built from basic logic circuits based on the ones Shannon proposed,
and has an architecture similar to the one von Neumann proposed.

Without the ideas of these three great scientists you could not be
reading this article on your phone, tablet, or computer, nor would I be
able to write it on my laptop, and perhaps our world would be completely
different. Either way, I'd love to be able to travel back in time and
peek through the windows of the Hay-Adams to watch these three
remarkable men talk\...
