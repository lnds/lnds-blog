By [Luis Ramírez](http://luisramirez.cl/blog/) (\*)

**What is the future of the Internet?** I am convinced that the [semantic
web](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_Web) is just a way of
deepening (improving?) how we relate to existing data. [The
Cloud](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing)?
More of the same. In my opinion, what will truly amount to a
significant innovation is taking the Internet out of traditional devices
and embedding it in unconventional ones. During this decade we will see
the rise of this second Internet, where anything at all becomes a "Smart
Object" able to communicate with the network at any time and from any
place. That, more than anything else, is where I believe the future of
the Internet lies.

## **2010-2020 will be the decade of the "Internet of Things"**

![](200809251302.jpg)

What would have to happen between now and 2020 is the mass adoption of
low-cost chips that can be embedded in almost anything (including
household objects like refrigerators, or vehicles) and applications
that allow not only two-way data transmission, but also give those
objects the ability to connect to the Internet.

This is a big leap that opens up unsuspected possibilities. My favorite
example is the toll tag we use on some highways in Chile. Today this
device is very basic. In the near future, it could trigger all kinds of
functions, whether in our homes, as we get close to the house, or with
service providers.

How do we move forward? Rolling out the new version of IP (version 6,
or IPv6) is no longer a matter of choice but of necessity. The current
situation, with IP addresses about to run out even under strict
management, means we are living through the last three or four years
before the "forced changeover".

[Leading companies worldwide are already working](http://www.ipso-alliance.org/Pages/Front.php) on that
transition through the "**IPSO Alliance**", which brings together 27 of
the industry's main players, including Intel, Sun, Cisco and
Google. The European Community's interest is also plain to see, while
pioneering nations like South Korea and Japan have been experimenting
for years. In short, everything indicates that the Internet of
Things is already under way worldwide.

**Are we ready in Chile?** Short answer: not for now, but we have
started to make progress. There are favorable factors and others that
complicate the picture a bit.

## **Favorable factors in Chile** 

**Widespread networks:** Not only do we have a reasonable deployment of
fixed networks, but mobile networks have grown at rates far above
expectations. On top of that, one operator (ENTEL) has taken on the
rural connectivity project, which means that by 2012-13 we will have
radically greater coverage in the regions and in isolated areas. In one
sentence: by 2014 Chile will have the best network infrastructure from
Mexico to Patagonia.

**IPv6 Project:** The initiative, led by NIC Labs with backing from
the Undersecretariat of Telecommunications (SUBTEL) and several
companies, has set out to design a "Strategic Plan" for Chile's
transition to IPv6. The goal is to have it in 30 months, so it is likely
that by mid-2012 we will have absolute clarity on how to start the
rollout.

**Interest from industry leaders:** During the early years of the
first Internet, companies were rather cautious, but the fact that they
are now actively taking part in the IPv6 project (for now Claro, Entel,
Movistar, Telmex, VTR and Cisco) looks like a good sign. Besides, these
and other players have reached sizes that let them experiment with new
technologies (LTE, for example) and bet a little more on innovation.

## **Limiting factors**

**Government and National Congress:** Here I think we have a major
bottleneck. Somebody correct me, but I haven't heard a single member of
Congress or minister mention the subject. As far as I know, the
President has never made any reference to it either. **Security and
Privacy:** As has been raised in international discussions, there is a
significant level of concern over the privacy of the data that will
circulate in this new ecosystem. It is likely that users' own misgivings
will limit the possible uses in the first few years, perhaps much as
happened when e-commerce took off.

## **Conclusion**

The implications of this article make no sense if you look at them from
a short-term perspective (2010). The underlying discussion has to be
framed across the entire decade. The important changes will come in no
less than five years. We have to do what we can so that it takes no
longer than that.

In the end, I'll stay with this quote, an optimistic one to be sure:
"Chile can take a leading position worldwide both in the software
development market and in the development of innovative products that
use the Internet as a means of communication between devices, as well as
being able to lead the implementation of IPv6 in the region." (IPv6
Project, NICLabs, Chile)

The question is not whether the "Internet of Things" will happen or not,
but how long the transition takes us. If we are more effective than we
were in rolling out the first Internet, in less than a decade Chile
could become a regional leader of this new Internet. For that to
happen, beyond chips and regulation, there is an enormous challenge
ahead for software developers and everyone involved in the computing
industry, which boils down to how to provide locally relevant
solutions that can adapt to devices of any kind.

Ubiquitous Internet also means ubiquitous software. That seems to be
where the future is coming from.
